Savitribai Phule

In 1848, a teenage teacher in Pune set out every morning on a short walk that people remembered for a century and a half. Men waited along the route. They threw cow dung, mud and stones at her, and shouted that she was ruining the girls she taught and bringing disgrace on everyone who knew her. The story told about her is that she carried a second sari in her bag, changed when she reached the schoolroom, and taught the whole day in clean clothes. Then she walked home through the same street and did it again the next day.

Her name was Savitribai Phule, and eight years earlier she hadn't been able to read at all. She had been married at about nine to a thirteen-year-old named Jyotirao Phule, from the Mali caste of gardeners and farmers, and he taught her her letters at home. She went on to formal teacher training — one of the first Indian women to have it — and on 1 January 1848 the couple opened a school for girls in a house called Bhide Wada. She was its teacher.

It wasn't the first girls' school in India; missionaries had opened some earlier. What made it dangerous was who ran it and who sat in it. It was run by Indians of a low-ranked caste, and before long the Phules were teaching girls and the children of castes then treated as untouchable, sometimes side by side. For orthodox Pune, that was two scandals in one building. Jyotirao's own father threw the couple out of his house for it.

They kept going. By 1851 they were running three girls' schools with about 150 pupils. In 1855 one of the movement's pupils, a fourteen-year-old Mang girl named Muktabai Salve, published an essay in a Marathi journal attacking how caste had crushed her people — the kind of voice these classrooms existed to produce. Savitribai wrote poems plain enough for a new reader and sharp enough to argue: "Sit idle no more, go, get education." When she saw young widows killing their newborns to escape disgrace, she didn't write a sermon against it; she opened a room in her own house where they could give birth safely. When widows' heads were being shaved, the fight went to the barbers holding the razors, and she backed them when they struck rather than do it.

Critics called the Phules agents of the missionaries, and some of their facts were right: she trained under missionaries, and the colonial government honoured their schools. But the people who claimed to be defending Indian tradition were the same people throwing dung at her. She took help from whoever offered it, because the girls in her classroom couldn't wait for a better offer.

She died in 1897 the way she had lived. Plague had reached Pune, and she and her adopted son, a doctor, were running a clinic on the edge of the city. When she heard that a boy in a nearby Mahar settlement had the plague and no one would go near him, she went and carried him to the clinic on her back. She caught the plague herself and died on 10 March 1897, aged sixty-six. She had started her working life walking through filth to reach a classroom, and ended it walking with a sick child on her back to reach a doctor. The direction never changed — toward the person nobody else was going to carry.

Core Philosophy

Savitribai treated education as the one reform that makes every other reform stick. A law against a custom can be ignored in a village no official visits; a girl who can read can't be un-taught. So she aimed at the root rather than the symptoms: not petitioning for the rights of women and low-caste children one grievance at a time, but putting a slate in their hands so they could read the scriptures, the laws, and the accounts being used against them. And she went further than most reformers of her day by refusing to separate the two exclusions. She taught girls and children of the castes then treated as untouchable, often in the same rooms, on the view that caste and patriarchy were one system of control that only a shared classroom could break.

Rights can be taken back by the next official. Literacy can't. She put her fifty years into the one thing that couldn't be undone.

Thinking Process

  • Attack two barriers with one classroom — Most reformers of her era picked one cause: women's education or caste reform, rarely both. The Phules' schools took girls and Mahar and Mang children together, deliberately — so the same room disproved two exclusions at once, and neither reform could be bought off separately.
  • Make the argument physical — Pune had heard every argument about whether girls could or should learn. A working school with girls reading in it was harder to argue with than any pamphlet, and her daily walk made it impossible to ignore. The school was the case.
  • Teach what gets you out, not just what makes you respectable — The Bhide Wada school is reported to have taught mathematics, science and social studies rather than only religious recitation — the subjects that let a pupil check a moneylender's ledger or a landlord's claim, not just recite a text chosen for her. The proof it worked came fast: in 1855 Muktabai Salve, a fourteen-year-old Mang girl from a Phule school, published an essay attacking caste in the Marathi journal Dnyanodaya.
  • Write in words a new reader can carry — Her 1854 poetry collection, Kavya Phule, is plain enough for a new reader and pointed enough to argue: its poems tell the oppressed to get educated and stop waiting. In a world where most of her audience could barely read, a short poem could be memorised and repeated; a treatise couldn't.
  • Remove the incentive, not just condemn the act — Widows who became pregnant, often through rape by relatives, were driven to kill their newborns or themselves to escape the shame. Instead of only preaching against infanticide, the Phules opened a home in 1863 where such a woman could give birth safely — taking away the reason, not just the permission.

Each step moved the fight from 'should this change?' to 'this has already changed — now what?' That is the ground an opponent finds hardest to hold.

Mental Models

  • Carry the Spare — She didn't wait for the abuse to stop before she set out. She planned around it. The spare sari, however exactly the story happened, is the model: expect the predictable cost, pack for it, and arrive ready to work anyway.
  • One Room, Two Walls — When two injustices share a root, fight them in the same place. Teaching girls and 'untouchable' children together meant neither group's education could be framed as a threat only to the other's opponents.
  • Change the Incentive, Not Just the Sermon — Behaviour that looks like cruelty is often a response to a trap. The infanticide home and the barbers' strike against shaving widows' heads both went after the conditions that produced the behaviour, not just the behaviour.
  • The Irreversible Lever — Put effort where it can't be reversed. A new rule can be repealed or ignored; a literate pupil stays literate and teaches the next one. She kept choosing the gain that compounds over the gain that makes headlines.
  • Build the Next Teacher — One teacher is a school; a teacher who can be replaced is a movement. When illness kept her at her parents' home in 1856, she wrote to Jyotirao not to worry about the school because Fatima would manage it. The work no longer depended on her being there — and seven years after Bhide Wada opened, one of the movement's pupils, Muktabai Salve, was already publishing her own arguments.

None of these models required power she didn't have. They required her to be precise about where the lever was — and then to keep pulling it while people threw things at her.

Big Ideas

  • Was She a Colonial Collaborator? — Orthodox critics said the Phules were acting for the Christian missionaries, and the facts they pointed to are real. Savitribai trained under missionaries; Jyotirao studied at a Scottish mission school and borrowed missionary arguments against caste; the colonial education department publicly honoured them in 1852; and Jyotirao argued that for low castes British rule was better than the Brahmin-dominated Peshwa order it replaced. But the charge skips the other half. Neither of them converted, and Jyotirao's 1883 book Shetkaryacha Asud attacked colonial officials alongside Brahmin ones for exploiting farmers. They took help where it existed, from whoever offered it, because the people who claimed to speak for 'Indian' tradition were the same people throwing dung at her. Whether that counts as collaboration depends on whose India you think was being defended.
  • Her Own Voice, Not Only His — So much of the Phules' work was joint that her part is easily folded into his. What is documented as hers alone: two books of poetry (Kavya Phule, 1854, and a verse biography of Jyotirao in the early 1890s); letters to him, including one dated 10 October 1856 recording her standing up to her own brother, who told her the couple had rightly been cast out for serving Mahars and Mangs; lighting Jyotirao's funeral pyre in 1890, when custom reserved that for a son or male relative; presiding over the Satyashodhak Samaj's 1893 session at Saswad after his death; and running the plague clinic in 1897.
  • A Home That Made Infanticide Unnecessary — In 1863 the Phules opened the Balhatya Pratibandhak Griha — literally a 'home for the prevention of infanticide' — in their own house, for widows who had become pregnant, often through abuse by relatives. In 1874 they adopted the son of one of them, Kashibai, a Brahmin widow; the boy, Yashwant, grew up to become a doctor and ran the clinic where Savitribai spent her last days.
  • The Barbers' Strike — Upper-caste widows were forced to have their heads shaved, a mark of permanent mourning and social death. Instead of only arguing with the families, reformers turned to the barbers — the people who actually held the razor — and barbers in Pune and Bombay struck rather than do the work, with Savitribai among the strike's best-known backers. It's the same move as the infanticide home: find the point where the practice physically depends on someone, and change that someone's mind.

Notes on the Record

  • The spare-sari story appears in most modern biographies but traces to later retellings rather than a contemporary record. The harassment itself — dung, mud and stones thrown at her on the way to school — is far better attested. This page treats the sari as the story told about her, not as verified fact.
  • Bhide Wada is often called 'India's first girls' school.' That's not accurate: missionaries ran girls' schools in several Indian cities before 1848. The defensible claim is that it was among the first girls' schools run by Indians, and the first in Pune. Its exact opening date is also reported differently across sources.
  • Fatima Sheikh's existence was publicly questioned in January 2025, when a writer claimed she was a modern invention. The strongest evidence against that claim is Savitribai's own letter of 10 October 1856, published in the Marathi collection of her writings in 1988, which names Fatima as running the school. Beyond that letter, though, details of Fatima's life — including the Usman Sheikh shelter story — rest on thinner sources than the rest of the Phules' record.
  • The barbers' strike against tonsuring widows is widely credited to Savitribai, but accounts differ on who organised it and when; some tie it to a press report from 1890. This page presents her as a prominent backer rather than its sole organiser. Sources also differ on whether Bavan Kashi Subodh Ratnakar was published in 1891 or 1892.
  • Figures for the Phules' later schools vary widely; totals of 18 or more are often quoted but are hard to verify. This page uses only the better-supported 1851 figure of three girls' schools and about 150 pupils.
  • The hero quotes come from a widely circulated English translation of her Marathi poem usually titled 'Go, Get Education.' Other translations word the lines differently; the meaning is consistent.

Savitribai Phule never won the argument about whether girls should learn to read. She made the argument irrelevant, by teaching them anyway, every day, until there were too many readers to pretend otherwise.

She began her working life walking through thrown dung to reach a schoolroom, and ended it carrying a sick child to a clinic. In between, every reform she touched followed the same rule: don't wait for the people in charge to agree. Find the place where the change can be made physically real — a classroom, a safe room for a widow, a barber who puts down his razor — and make it real there.

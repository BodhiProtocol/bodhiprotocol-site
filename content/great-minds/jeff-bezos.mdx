In 1994, Jeff Bezos was 30 years old, the youngest vice president at a successful Wall Street quantitative hedge fund, with a stable career most people would never willingly walk away from. He'd noticed something in the fund's own research: internet usage was growing at over 2,300 percent a year, a number large enough that almost nothing built to serve that growth yet existed. He didn't run a spreadsheet on the odds of success. He used a simpler test: imagine himself at 80, looking back. Would he regret having tried this and failed? Or would he regret never having tried at all? The math wasn't close. He quit, and drove from New York to Seattle, writing a business plan in the passenger seat along the way.

He started with books — not because he loved books particularly, but because books solved a specific structural problem. No physical store, however large, could stock more than a fraction of the millions of titles in print, but an online store had no shelf limit at all. Books were cheap enough that a nervous first-time customer might risk an unfamiliar new way of buying something. And an existing wholesale distribution network already existed to build against, so Amazon didn't need to invent supply chains from nothing. The store wasn't really about books. It was a wedge, chosen for its structural advantages, aimed at a much larger idea Bezos wouldn't call "the everything store" out loud for years.

The company he built out of that decision has spent three decades operating on a premise most public companies structurally can't afford: that almost nobody is willing to wait as long as Amazon is willing to wait. Amazon Web Services took years to become one of the most profitable businesses in the world. Prime took years to become a habit. Every annual letter to shareholders since 1997 has reprinted the original letter alongside the new one, as a standing reminder of a specific belief — that the moment a company stops behaving like it's still Day 1, decline isn't a risk. It's already begun.

That willingness to wait — for years, sometimes over a decade, on a bet almost no competitor is structured to match — is the thread running through everything else on this page.

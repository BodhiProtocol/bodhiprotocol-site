Think of planning a wedding.

You don't estimate how long the caterer needs, then the tailor, then the hall booking, and add it all up to see where you land. You start from the date already printed on invitations mailed to three hundred people, and work every vendor's deadline backward from that one immovable day — because the day itself was never up for negotiation.

In December 1964, a cyclone tore the Pamban Bridge apart — the two-kilometre rail crossing over open sea that was Rameswaram's only link to the mainland. Girders lay twisted in the water. The Railway Board, assessing the wreckage, put the repair at roughly six months. A young engineer named E. Sreedharan looked at the same wreckage and promised 46 days — then spent them on site around the clock, timing every crew's work to the tide charts, because the sea only allowed certain hours of access at all. He made the deadline with days to spare.

That single bridge turned out to be a preview of an entire career. Thirty years later, as Chairman of the newly formed Konkan Railway Corporation, he took on a 760-kilometre line through the Western Ghats that four decades of surveys had already called too difficult and too expensive to build — and delivered it in under eight years, paid for with public bonds he raised himself rather than waiting on a government budget cycle. Then came the Delhi Metro, where "on schedule" stopped being an aspiration and became simply what happened, section after section, for over fifteen years.

Set beside this, most large infrastructure projects run the opposite way: estimate every stage forward, add the estimates up, call the total a deadline, then quietly push the date back when the estimate turns out wrong somewhere — which, on a project this size, it almost always does. Berlin's Brandenburg Airport took nine years longer than planned. Boston's Big Dig ran a decade over schedule. Delay of this kind is treated almost like weather: unfortunate, structural, nobody's fault in particular.

Sreedharan worked backward instead. He fixed the finish date first — sometimes before a single tunnel was dug — and reverse-engineered every contract, every buffer, every crew's schedule from that one fixed point. Run a project this way, and a slipping task stops being an item for next month's status report. It becomes an alarm today, because there was never any slack left at the far end to quietly absorb it later.

In 1955, an American watchmaker named Bulova offered a small, barely known Japanese company an order worth several times its entire capital — 100,000 transistor radios, on one condition: sell them under the Bulova name, not the maker's own. Akio Morita turned it down. "Fifty years from now," he told Bulova's buyer, "I promise you our name will be just as famous as your company's name is today." The company was Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo. Within three years, it renamed itself Sony.

That refusal sits inside a longer pattern that runs through almost everything Morita built. He didn't wait for evidence that a product would sell before shipping it — he shrank the product until it fit somewhere it hadn't before, and treated the resulting demand as something he'd created, not discovered. A suitcase-sized tape recorder in 1950 became a radio that fit two hands by 1955, then one that fit a shirt pocket by 1957, then a television one person could carry by 1960, then, by 1979, a stereo music player with no speaker at all — built for one set of ears, over the explicit objections of his own marketing department.

This page is about that instinct: not miniaturization as an engineering trick, but as a repeatable bet that the next size down was worth building before anyone had asked for it.

Think of two ways to run a coffee shop.

In the first, you brew a fixed number of cups every fifteen minutes, on schedule, whether or not anyone is standing at the counter to buy them. In the second, you brew a new cup only after someone has actually ordered one, and you only restock beans once your existing supply visibly runs low.

Henry Ford ran the first kind of shop, at the scale of an entire industry, for four decades. Taiichi Ohno built the second kind — and it took him nearly thirty years to get it right.

Ford's moving assembly line, the one already covered elsewhere on this site, pushed parts forward on a fixed schedule: the conveyor moved at a set pace, and the worker's job was to keep up with it, regardless of whether a dealership somewhere actually wanted the finished car waiting at the end of the line. It was extraordinarily fast. It was not, in Ohno's later view, actually efficient — because speed alone says nothing about whether you're building things anyone currently wants.

In 1956, Ohno traveled to the United States specifically to study Ford's methods. He came home unimpressed by the factories and oddly captivated by supermarkets — specifically, by how a shelf only got restocked after a customer had actually taken something off it. That observation became kanban: a simple card that travels backward through a factory, signaling exactly how much of something has been used and exactly how much more to make. Nothing more, and — just as importantly — nothing less.

The tidy version of this story ends there: one supermarket trip, one insight, one system. The real version is less convenient. Toyota's own internal pull-system experiments were already running years before that trip. What the visit actually did was confirm and accelerate work already in progress — which is a much better description of how the whole Toyota Production System actually came together: not a single flash of genius, but three decades of small, continuously tested revisions, most of them invisible outside the factory where they happened.

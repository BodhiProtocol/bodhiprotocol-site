corePhilosophy: "Tagore distrusted walls of every kind — the classroom wall, the language wall, the wall of empire, and, most controversially, the wall of the nation. His argument was that each wall starts as protection and ends as a prison: the school built to educate that trains children to repeat; the nation built to resist an empire that learns to hate like one. He didn't want walls replaced by nothing. He wanted them replaced by relationships — between teacher and pupil, between India and the world, between people and the land they farmed. That put him on the wrong side of almost everyone at some point: the British, whose honour he returned; the nationalists, whose boycotts he criticised; and even Gandhi, whose certainty he questioned in public."

    icon: "Scale"
    description: "Critics called him an aristocrat — he was a landowner's son who managed his family's estates — telling a mass movement to slow down while people were being shot. There's force in that: mass non-cooperation needed discipline, and India won independence partly through the unquestioning commitment Tagore worried about. But his defenders point to what came later. Rabindranath Tagore hated school. At seven he was sent to one in Calcutta and found a cramped room, a teacher reading out lessons, and boys made to repeat them until they were memorised. His family moved him to another school, and then another, and another. He hardly ever went. At fifteen he left the last one for good. He never earned a degree.

He went on to become the most famous writer India has ever produced. He wrote more than two thousand songs, including the national anthems of two countries, and in 1913 he became the first person from outside Europe to win the Nobel Prize in Literature. And in 1901, the boy who couldn't bear a classroom opened his own school, at Santiniketan in rural Bengal. It had about five pupils, and lessons took place outdoors, under the trees.

That school is the easiest way into how Tagore thought. He believed walls — the kind around a classroom, and the kind around a language, an empire or a nation — usually start out as protection and end up as prisons. So he spent his life taking them down. When nobody outside Bengal could read his poems, he translated them into English himself. When the British massacred unarmed Indians at Jallianwala Bagh in 1919, he handed back the knighthood they had given him four years earlier. And at sixty-three, when most people retire, he started painting, and made around 2,500 pictures before he died.

The wall that got him into the most trouble was the nation. Tagore wanted India free, and he said so. But he warned, in lectures in 1916 and 1917, that nationalism itself — a people organised around hating an enemy — was 'a great menace.' He argued in print with Gandhi, whom he deeply admired, about burning foreign cloth and spinning cotton as a patriotic duty. When Gandhi said a terrible earthquake in Bihar was God's punishment for untouchability, Tagore told him plainly that earthquakes have physical causes, not moral ones.

Many people fighting for independence found him infuriating: a rich man's son telling a movement to slow down while its members were being beaten and shot. They had a point, and the page below takes it seriously. But the question he kept asking — what is this wall protecting, and what is it stopping us from learning? — is one that works on nearly everything, from a school timetable to a national border. He started asking it as a miserable boy in a classroom, and he was still asking it, in his last public speech, at eighty.

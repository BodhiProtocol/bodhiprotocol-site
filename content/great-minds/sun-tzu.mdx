According to the historian Sima Qian, writing some four centuries after the fact, King Helü of Wu once challenged Sun Tzu to prove that his theories of war applied to anyone — even the 180 women of the royal palace. Sun Tzu accepted, divided the women into two companies, and put the king's two favorite concubines in command of each. He explained the drill commands clearly. When he gave the order, the women laughed.

Sun Tzu said the fault was his — the instructions must not have been clear enough — and repeated them. He gave the order again. They laughed again. This time, he said, the fault belonged to the officers who had failed to enforce a clear command, and ordered the two company commanders executed on the spot. The king, watching from a tower, sent an urgent message asking him to spare his favorites. Sun Tzu's reported reply was that a general in the field is not always bound by the sovereign's orders. He had them beheaded, appointed new commanders from the ranks, and gave the drill order a third time. The companies moved in perfect silence, exactly as instructed.

The story may be part legend — Sun Tzu's very existence is debated by historians, and the text attributed to him may have been assembled by several hands over a century or more. But the story survives because it captures something the text itself argues constantly: that method, not rank or reputation, is what actually wins. An order everyone knows will be enforced doesn't need to be repeated. A war whose outcome has already been calculated in advance barely needs to be fought.

That distinction — between what looks like strength and what has actually been prepared — is the rest of this page.

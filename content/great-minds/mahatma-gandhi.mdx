Every other nonviolent protest of the twentieth century borrowed something from a method that Mohandas Gandhi spent the better part of fifty-five years building, testing, and re-testing at increasing scale. He didn't arrive at satyagraha as a finished idea. He backed into it on a train platform in Pietermaritzburg in 1893, spent the next two decades drilling it on a small expatriate community in South Africa, and only then brought it home to India — where he ran it again, deliberately smaller before larger, on indigo farmers in Champaran before he ever pointed it at the British Empire directly.

The through-line is easy to miss because the scale changes so much between one end of his life and the other. A single barrister refusing to give up a train seat and a 240-mile march that produced some of the most reproduced photographs in the history of protest look like different orders of event. They ran on the same mechanism: absorb the state's violence without returning it, force the injustice into daylight, and let the resulting moral asymmetry do the political work that force never could.

What made the method scale wasn't charisma alone. It was that Gandhi built a version of it almost anyone could personally participate in — a spinning wheel turned for twenty minutes a day, cloth worn instead of imported cloth bought — long before he ever asked the country for something as costly as a 240-mile march or a prison sentence. The wheel he chose as the movement's literal symbol was also, structurally, how the movement itself was built: the same simple motion, turned at a slightly wider radius, again and again, for over four decades.

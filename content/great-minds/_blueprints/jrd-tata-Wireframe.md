## Opening (MDX lede — non-biographical, no "X was born...")
Opens on the 1978 removal, not his birth: in 1953 the Indian government nationalized Air India and immediately named JRD its first chairman anyway; his authority survived that. It took Prime Minister Morarji Desai's direct decision in 1978 — twenty-five years later — to actually end it. Pivots into: that gap between "who owns it" and "who's actually trusted to run it" is the real subject of the page, not the size of the group he built.

## Core Philosophy
**Philosophy:** His real skill wasn't running the Tata Group — it was arranging for other people to run it better than he could have alone. Under his 53-year chairmanship the group grew from roughly a dozen companies to nearly a hundred, not through his own hands-on management but by finding leaders he trusted and staking his authority on their judgment. The state could take ownership of Air India away from the Tata family in 1953; its own answer was still to hand him the chairmanship.
**Takeaway:** He didn't hold onto power. He held onto trust — and trust turned out to be harder to take away.

## Thinking Process (6 steps)
1. **Learn to fly, then stop flying** — neighbor of Louis Blériot as a child, first Indian commercial pilot's license (1929), personally flew Tata Aviation Service's first airmail run (1932) — then spent the rest of his career handing real control to someone else.
2. **Give away the whole cockpit, not a seat in it** — Mody (Tata Steel), Moolgaokar (Telco), Seth (Tata Chemicals), Kohli (TCS) each ran their company with genuine operational autonomy, not as a reporting division.
3. **Bind the federation with trust, not just equity** — alignment came from personal relationships and shared values more than Tata Sons' formal ownership stake.
4. **Build welfare before the law requires it** — eight-hour day, free medical aid, provident scheme, accident compensation, introduced well ahead of statutory requirement.
5. **Let the state take ownership, not authority** — nationalization in 1953 didn't sideline him; the government named him Air India's first chairman under state ownership.
6. **Accept that only a direct decision, not drift, ends real trust** — the chairmanship wasn't eroded by circumstance; it ended because Morarji Desai deliberately chose to end it in 1978.
**Takeaway:** He spent five decades proving a title survives an ownership change just fine. It takes an actual decision to end it.

## Closing Reflection
He built an airline, then gave up flying it. He built a company, then gave up running every piece of it. Even the one thing that should have been unrepeatable — his standing atop Air India — outlived the government taking it away from his family. The group he left behind in 1991 was nearly a hundred companies deep, none of them run by his own hand.

Ownership, it turns out, is easy to transfer. What he actually built was harder to take: the kind of trust that survives a change of ownership, and only ends when somebody spends the will to end it directly.

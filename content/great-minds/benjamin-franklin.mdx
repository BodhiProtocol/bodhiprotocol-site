Benjamin Franklin's first attempt at moral perfection lasted about as long as most New Year's resolutions. Sometime around 1730, he drew up a list of twelve virtues he intended to master — Temperance, Silence, Order, Resolution, and eight more — and set out to practice all of them at once, all the time, by sheer force of will. It went the way such projects usually go. He'd catch himself failing at Order while trying to fix Silence, and failing at Silence while trying to fix Temperance, and within weeks the old habits had quietly moved back in.

So he rebuilt the project as a system instead of a resolution. He ruled a small book into thirteen pages, one per virtue, each with a one-line definition, and beneath it a grid: seven columns for the days of the week, thirteen rows for the virtues, with one virtue given the full week's undivided attention while the other twelve were merely monitored. A black dot went in the box for every fault, no matter how small. Thirteen weeks completed one full pass through the list; he ran the cycle four times a year, for years. A Quaker friend also told him around this time that he was generally thought proud in argument — so he added a thirteenth virtue, Humility, whose entire instruction to himself read: "Imitate Jesus and Socrates."

He never got the ledger clean. Order, he admitted decades later in his *Autobiography*, defeated him for life — he simply wasn't a naturally tidy person, and no amount of weekly scrutiny fixed that. But he also concluded the attempt had made him a better and happier man regardless of whether it ever reached perfection. The system, not the outcome, was the point — and that's the thread running through nearly everything else he built.

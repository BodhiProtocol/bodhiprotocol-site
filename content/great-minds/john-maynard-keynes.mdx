corePhilosophy: "Keynes trained inside classical economics and spent his most productive years arguing against its central article of faith: that markets, left alone, would always clear. Say's Law held that supply creates its own demand — produce a good, and the income paid to make it would eventually be spent buying something, so a general glut of unsold goods and idle workers couldn't persist for long. Keynes' General Theory (1936) argued the opposite was not only possible but, in the Depression, was actually happening: wages and prices could stay 'sticky' instead of adjusting downward, businesses could sit on cash instead of investing it, and an economy could settle into a stable equilibrium well below full employment — not a temporary friction on its way to correcting itself, but a resting point it could occupy for years. If the private sector wouldn't spend enough to close that gap, Keynes argued, government could and should, financed by borrowing if necessary, because the multiplier meant that spending would work harder than its face value implied."

Think of a friend who picks up a ₹2,000 dinner bill for the table. The waiter takes a share home as a tip and spends part of it at the local kirana store; the shopkeeper deposits part of that sale and uses the rest to pay a supplier; the supplier's delivery driver spends part of his cut on fuel. Nobody at that dinner table intended to support the whole street's economy for the week. One bill kept circulating through hands long after the meal itself was paid for and forgotten.

That circulation — money spent, re-spent, and re-spent again before it finally leaks out as someone's savings, tax payment, or import bill — is the mechanism John Maynard Keynes turned into the most consequential idea in twentieth-century economics: the multiplier. And he needed it, because the economics he'd been trained in couldn't explain the Depression sitting outside his window.

Classical economists — Adam Smith's intellectual descendants — had spent over a century arguing that markets self-correct: prices and wages adjust, supply eventually finds its own demand, and any unemployment is a temporary friction that clears itself given enough time. Keynes' reply, in a single sentence that outlived the technical argument that produced it, was that "in the long run we are all dead." Waiting for wages to fall far enough to clear the labor market wasn't a neutral policy — it was a policy that left people unemployed for years while economists waited for a self-correction that, across Britain and the United States through the 1920s and '30s, simply wasn't arriving on schedule.

His fix wasn't subtle. If households and businesses wouldn't spend enough to keep the economy at full employment, government could spend on their behalf — and because of the multiplier, every pound of that spending would recirculate through the economy several times over before it finally leaked away, doing more work than its face value alone would suggest. It's the same logic, two decades later and half a world away, behind P.C. Mahalanobis's investment-multiplier model in India's Second Five-Year Plan, and behind every government's decision since, from 2008 to the COVID-19 pandemic, to answer a collapse in demand with a stimulus package rather than patience.

Charles Wheatstone asked Ada Lovelace to translate a French paper. That was the whole assignment: render Italian engineer Luigi Menabrea's account of Charles Babbage's unbuilt Analytical Engine into English, for readers of a British scientific journal who couldn't read the original. It was useful, technical, and entirely unremarkable work.

She didn't stop at translating. Over nine months in 1843, working mostly by letter with Babbage himself, she appended her own notes — lettered A through G — that ran to nearly three times the length of Menabrea's paper. The last of them, Note G, laid out an exact, step-by-step sequence of operations for the Engine to compute a series of Bernoulli numbers. No machine existed yet that could run it. None would exist for another ninety-nine years. The algorithm was correct the entire time; it just had nothing to execute it on.

What made Note G more than an unusually thorough footnote was what Lovelace saw underneath it. Babbage had designed a calculator, built to replace the human "computers" — a job title, then, not yet a machine — who tabulated logarithms and navigational tables by hand, one error-prone entry at a time. Lovelace looked at the same design, its instructions fed on cards borrowed directly from the Jacquard loom's punched-card weaving system, and described it differently: a machine that operated on symbols according to rules, where numbers just happened to be the first symbols anyone had fed it. If pitch and harmony could be written down as precisely as arithmetic, she argued, the Engine could compose music. She was equally precise about where that power stopped — the machine, she wrote, "has no pretensions whatever to originate anything." It could only do exactly what it was told, however far that turned out to reach.

She died at 36, in 1852, of uterine cancer — the same age her father, Lord Byron, was when he died, a coincidence she'd have appreciated more than most. Her notes sat mostly unread for a century, until a 1953 reprint put them back in front of readers building the first machines actually capable of running what she'd specified. By then the question she'd raised almost as an aside — whether a machine that only executes instructions can ever be said to originate anything — had become one Alan Turing felt he had to answer directly, in the paper that opened the field of artificial intelligence. She'd named the boundary before there was a machine capable of testing it.

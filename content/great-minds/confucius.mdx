For thirteen years, Confucius packed up his household and moved from court to court, pitching a program of government built on cultivated character to one ruler after another. Every one of them said no. He was in his sixties when he gave up and went home to Lu, spending what was left of his life teaching a shrinking circle of students and editing texts he hadn't written.

By any measure available to him at the time, he'd failed at the one thing he'd set out to do. He never governed a state. He never got close.

What he left behind instead was a sequence — investigate, know, intend, rectify, cultivate, and only then regulate, govern, and finally reach everyone under heaven — and three and a half centuries of nobody testing it at scale. When the Han dynasty finally did, in 136 BCE, it wasn't because Confucius had convinced anyone in his own lifetime. It was because the argument itself was still standing long after every ruler who'd rejected it was gone.

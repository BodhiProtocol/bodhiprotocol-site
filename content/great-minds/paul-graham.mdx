Paul Graham has a habit that looks almost careless: he throws away his best sentences. Not the bad ones — those are easy to cut. The good ones. The clever turn of phrase, the line that took an hour to get right. If it doesn't carry the argument forward, it goes, no matter how much it cost to write.

That habit didn't start with essays. It runs through everything he's built — the sparse Lisp code that ran Viaweb, the short list of rules that became Y Combinator's actual playbook, even a programming language reduced to its bare axioms. Graham's real subject was never startups or software. It was how much you can take away from an idea before it stops being true.

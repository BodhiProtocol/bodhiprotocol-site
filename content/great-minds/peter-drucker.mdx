In 1943, General Motors did something no large American corporation had ever done: it opened its internal meetings, its executive committee sessions, and its plant floors to an outside observer with no stake in the outcome and no obligation to say anything flattering. The observer was a 33-year-old Austrian-born economist named Peter Drucker, and GM's chairman, Alfred Sloan, expected a study of how a modern corporation should be organized.

He got that — and something Sloan hadn't asked for. Drucker's 1946 book, *Concept of the Corporation*, praised GM's decentralized structure as a genuine innovation, then went further: it argued the corporation was a social institution, not just an economic machine, with obligations to the people and communities it employed that went well beyond payroll. Sloan was reportedly unmoved — he later wrote his own book largely to correct the record — and GM shelved most of what it didn't already agree with.

The rest of the corporate world didn't. Over the following decade, decentralization stopped being a GM peculiarity and became the default architecture of the large American company. And Drucker, having been let inside one organization for eighteen months, spent the next sixty years doing the same thing to nearly every kind of organization he could get access to — companies, hospitals, universities, the Girl Scouts of the USA — convinced that management wasn't an instinct a handful of gifted executives happened to have. It was a discipline, learnable and teachable, and someone needed to write it down.

That distinction — between an organization that happens to be well run and one being run on purpose — is the rest of this page.

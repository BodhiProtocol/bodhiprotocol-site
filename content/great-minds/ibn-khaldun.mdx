In 1401, a chief judge in Mamluk Cairo was lowered by rope over the walls of a besieged Damascus to go negotiate with the man laying siege to it. Ibn Khaldun spent the following seven weeks in conversation with Timur — Tamerlane — talking history, geography, and the Maghreb with a conqueror who was, in that exact moment, building the kind of dynasty Ibn Khaldun had spent the previous two decades theorizing about. It's one of the strangest meetings in medieval history: a man who had just written down why every dynasty eventually collapses, sitting across from a man actively founding one.

By then, Ibn Khaldun had already lived the pattern he described more times than most historians ever get to observe directly. Courts in Tunis, Fez, and Granada had employed him and then dismissed him, imprisoned him, and reshuffled him across the political map of 14th-century North Africa and Andalusia. In 1375, rather than take another court post, he withdrew with his family to a fortress in the Algerian desert and spent four years writing down what two decades of watching dynasties rise and fall had taught him: that the loyalty holding any state together — what he called 'asabiyyah — has a predictable lifespan, roughly three or four generations, regardless of which family happens to be on the throne when it finally runs out.

This page is about that theory, and about a career that gave him an unusually direct, often uncomfortable vantage point from which to build it — six terms as a judge, at least one imprisonment, and one memorable rope over a city wall.

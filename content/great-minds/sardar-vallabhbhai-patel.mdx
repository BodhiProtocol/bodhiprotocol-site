Think of a landlord who owns three hundred separate buildings, each with its own tenant holding a lease that lets them stay, leave, or rent from someone else entirely — and the landlord has about two years to convince nearly all of them to sign with him, using whatever argument actually works on each one.

That, at a scale of nations rather than buildings, was Sardar Patel's task the day India became independent.

On 15 August 1947, British paramountcy over India's princely states simply lapsed. Each of the roughly 562 states — some smaller than a large farm, one larger than England and Scotland combined — became, for a moment, legally free to join India, join Pakistan, or remain sovereign. Nothing in the law required any of them to choose India. If sixty or a hundred of the larger ones had chosen otherwise, the country now familiar on a map would never have existed in that shape.

Patel didn't have a single argument that worked on all of them. So he didn't build one. For most rulers, the Instrument of Accession offered a straightforward trade: hand over defence, foreign affairs, and communications to Delhi, keep internal autonomy and a guaranteed income for life, and avoid the obvious math problem of trying to survive alone as a landlocked territory in a subcontinent that had just redrawn its own borders. Hundreds signed within days.

The handful who didn't sign got a different answer each. Junagadh's ruler had acceded to Pakistan against the wishes of a population that was overwhelmingly Hindu — so Patel let a plebiscite settle it instead of an argument. Kashmir's Maharaja was still undecided when an armed invasion from Pakistan forced his hand, and he signed in exchange for immediate military help. Hyderabad — the richest, largest, most defiant holdout of all — got neither incentive nor a vote. After more than a year of negotiation went nowhere, it got five days of military action, and then it, too, signed.

Three different problems. Three different tools. One deadline, applied to all of them at once, that made staying undecided visibly costly to everyone watching.

The closest historical parallel outside India makes the pace look even sharper. Otto von Bismarck spent nearly a decade, and three separate wars, consolidating thirty-odd German states into a unified empire in 1871. Patel folded more than five hundred sovereign territories — some larger than the German states Bismarck fought over — into one union in about two years, with a single sustained military campaign lasting five days. The difference wasn't that his task was smaller. It was that incentive did almost all of the work Bismarck needed war for, and force stayed the last resort rather than the first one.

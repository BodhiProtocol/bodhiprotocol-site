Think of a locksmith called in to open a jammed door.

A good locksmith uses exactly enough force to pop the lock — no more. Someone who keeps swinging the crowbar after the door is already open isn't being thorough. They're breaking the door frame, the hinges, and their own way back through it later.

Otto von Bismarck ran nineteenth-century Europe's most consequential unification project the same way, and it's the second half of that sentence — knowing when to stop swinging — that made him unusual.

Between 1864 and 1871, Prussia fought three wars, against Denmark, then Austria, then France, and unified the German states into a single empire. Each war was fast: weeks or months, not years. Each had a specific, narrow objective decided in advance. And each one stopped exactly when that objective was met — including the moment in 1866 when Bismarck's own king wanted to keep going, and Bismarck talked him out of it.

After Prussia's crushing win over Austria at Königgrätz, with Vienna essentially undefended, King Wilhelm I wanted the full spoils: a victory parade through the Austrian capital, a war indemnity, annexed territory. Bismarck refused, arguing against his own monarch and military command until he won. The peace took no Austrian land at all. He could already see four years ahead, to a war with France that Prussia would need Austria to simply stay out of — and a humiliated Austria was far more likely to look for revenge than a merely defeated one.

That instinct — stop the moment the job is done, whatever more you could still take — explains both halves of his career, not just the military one. Once Germany existed in 1871, Bismarck fought no more wars at all. He spent the next nineteen years doing something close to the opposite of everything that had built the country: patient, unglamorous alliance-building, aimed at a single goal — making sure France could never assemble the coalition it would need for a war of revenge. It worked for as long as he held the job. Four years after a new Kaiser dismissed him and let the alliances lapse, that carefully built structure had already come apart.

corePhilosophy: "The Buddha's starting point was not God, the soul or the creation of the world — questions he often declined to answer as unhelpful. It was a practical problem every person meets: life keeps falling short, and we keep reacting by grasping harder. His answer was structured like a medical case. Describe the condition precisely. Trace it to a cause you can actually act on. Establish that a cure is possible. Prescribe a treatment that people can follow. And then test it on yourself, because the teaching was explicitly offered as something to be practised and verified, not merely believed. He also taught within the worldview of his time — rebirth and karma run through the early texts — so the purely psychological Buddha of modern books is a selection from his teaching, not the whole of it."
corePhilosophyTakeaway: "Don't start with the questions you can't answer. Start with the problem in front of you, find its cause, and treat that."
thinkingProcess:
  - title: "Test the extremes, then reject both"
    description: "He tried a life of pleasure and a life of self-torture, and found neither ended dissatisfaction. The 'middle way' wasn't a compromise picked in advance; it was a conclusion reached by running both experiments to failure."
  - title: "Diagnose before you prescribe"
    description: "The four truths go in a fixed order: the problem, its cause, the possibility of a cure, the cure. Early texts compare this to a skilled physician who knows the illness, its origin, its cure and how to prevent its return. Skipping straight to advice is how most self-help fails."
  - title: "Put aside questions that don't help"
    description: "Asked whether the universe is eternal or what happens to an awakened person after death, he often declined to answer. In one famous simile, a man shot by a poisoned arrow refuses treatment until he knows who shot it and what the bow was made of — and dies waiting."
  - title: "Let people find the truth themselves"
    description: "In a much-loved story, a young mother named Kisā Gotamī carried her dead child from house to house, begging for medicine. The Buddha told her he could help if she brought him mustard seed from a home where no one had ever died. She found no such home — and understood, in a way no lecture could have taught her, that death comes to every family. (The story comes from commentaries written centuries later, but it captures how he taught: by giving people an experience, not an instruction.)"
  - title: "Make it checkable"
    description: "He told the Kālāmas, a village confused by competing teachers, not to accept a teaching merely because of tradition, scripture or a teacher's authority, but to judge it by whether it increases or reduces greed, hatred and delusion when put into practice."
  - title: "Build an institution to carry it"
    description: "He didn't just teach individuals. He founded the saṅgha, a community of monks and later nuns with written rules, open to people regardless of caste — the structure that carried the teaching across Asia long after his death."
thinkingProcessTakeaway: "Each step moves from opinion to evidence: try it, observe the result, keep what works. That is why the method travelled so well — it could be tested in any culture."

bigIdeas:
  - title: "No Fixed Self: His Most Original Idea"
    icon: "Brain"
    description: "Think of a river. It keeps its name for centuries, but the water in it is never the same from one moment to the next. The Buddha said a person is like that. Many thinkers of his time, including the Upanishads, taught that behind the changing body and mind lies a permanent true self, the ātman. In his second teaching he argued the opposite: look at every part of a person — body, feelings, perceptions, intentions, consciousness — and none of it is permanent or fully under our control, so none of it can be a fixed self. This is anattā, 'non-self'. It sits on top of a wider idea, dependent origination, which he summed up as: 'When this is, that is. From the arising of this, that arises. When this is not, that is not. From the ceasing of this, that ceases.' Everything — including you — is a process that arises from causes, not a thing that simply exists."
  - title: "What He Argued Against"
    icon: "Scale"
    description: "He was one of the śramaṇas — wandering seekers who left home and challenged the authority of the Brahmin priests and their Vedic rituals. He rejected caste by birth in blunt terms: 'Not by birth is one an outcast; not by birth is one a brāhmin. By deeds one becomes an outcast; by deeds one becomes a brāhmin.' He admitted people of every caste to his order. And he opposed animal sacrifice: in one discourse, a Brahmin planning to slaughter hundreds of animals is persuaded to hold a 'sacrifice' of generosity instead, made with nothing but ghee, oil, butter, curds and honey. He also turned away from the other extreme of his day — the harsh self-starvation many śramaṇas practised — which he had tried and found useless."
  - title: "From India to Asia — and Back Again"
    icon: "Globe"
    description: "About two centuries after his death, the emperor Aśoka sent Buddhist teachers out from India, and over the following centuries the teaching spread to Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia, China, Korea, Japan and Tibet. In India itself it slowly faded; the sacking of the great monastic university at Nālandā around 1193 is often named as one blow among several. Then, on 14 October 1956 in Nagpur, B.R. Ambedkar — the architect of India's Constitution — converted to Buddhism with several hundred thousand followers, many of them from communities treated as untouchable, choosing it precisely because the Buddha had rejected caste by birth. Today hundreds of millions of people around the world identify as Buddhist."
  - title: "How Much Do We Actually Know About Him?"
    icon: "AlertTriangle"
    description: "Less than most people assume. His teachings were memorised and recited for centuries; the Pāli texts were first written down in Sri Lanka in the 1st century BCE, roughly four hundred years after his death. Even his dates are uncertain: the traditional 563–483 BCE has given way, for many scholars, to a death around 400 BCE. The strongest physical evidence is a pillar at Lumbini put up around 249 BCE by Aśoka — the Mauryan emperor who ruled most of the subcontinent and sent Buddhist teachers across Asia — recording that he visited 'the place where Buddha Śākyamuni was born'. Everything on this page is the Buddha of the early texts — the best evidence we have, but not a biography."
  - title: "Women in the Order: Radical, and Complicated"
    icon: "Scale"
    description: "He admitted women as fully ordained nuns, beginning, according to the texts, with Mahāpajāpatī — his mother's sister, who had raised him — a radical step in his time and place. But the same account says he hesitated three times and admitted them only with eight extra rules (the garudhammas) placing every nun below every monk. Modern scholars have found serious problems with this story: Ānanda, who supposedly pleaded for the women, had by other parts of the texts' own chronology not yet become a monk when the request was made, and other passages describe women ordained by the Buddha directly. Many now think the eight rules were added later. The honest position is that both the openness and the restrictions are in the record, and historians still argue about which came first."
  - title: "The Fake Buddha Quotes"
    icon: "Sparkles"
    description: "'Holding on to anger is like grasping a hot coal… you are the one who gets burned' is not in any early text. A similar image appears in Buddhaghosa's 5th-century commentary, written about nine hundred years after the Buddha, and a 1987 self-help book put it in the Buddha's mouth. 'Believe nothing, no matter who said it, unless it agrees with your own reason' is a distortion of the Kālāma discourse, which actually says reason alone isn't enough — test teachings by their effects. And the jolly, round 'Laughing Buddha' statue is Budai, a Chinese monk of around the 10th century, not Gautama at all."
  - title: "The 'Secular Buddha' Problem"
    icon: "Brain"
    description: "Modern readers often meet the Buddha as a meditation teacher or an early psychologist, with rebirth and karma quietly removed. The early texts don't support that cut: rebirth and the moral consequences of action across lifetimes run through them, and they frame the whole goal of ending the cycle. Secular practitioners argue the practical core stands without them; traditional Buddhists argue that removing them changes what the path is for. Both sides have textual support. This page focuses on his method, but it doesn't pretend he taught only that."

Imagine a boy raised in a palace where, the story goes, nothing sad was ever allowed in. No one old, no one sick, no funerals. Then one day, as a young man, he went outside and saw an old man bent over a stick, a sick man lying in the road, and a dead body being carried away. Nobody had told him that this happens to everyone.

That story comes from texts written long after his death, and we can't know if it's true. What the earliest texts do agree on is what he did next. At about 29, Siddhārtha Gautama — a prince of the Śākya people, born in what is now southern Nepal — left his wife, his newborn son and his inheritance, and set out to understand why life keeps falling short of what we want from it.

He tried the most extreme answer on offer first. For about six years he fasted and punished his body, until, the texts say, touching his stomach meant touching his spine. It got him nowhere. So he did something his companions found unforgivable: he ate. They walked out. He sat down under a tree, and by his own account, he understood.

What he understood, he laid out like a doctor. There is a problem: life is shot through with dissatisfaction. It has a cause: craving — wanting things to be other than they are. It can end. And here is the treatment: a practical eight-part path of ethics, mental training and understanding. He said he taught only two things, 'suffering and the ending of suffering', and he told people not to take his word for it but to test the treatment on themselves.

Two more of his ideas were radical for his time. He taught that there is no fixed, permanent self inside you — a person is more like a river than a stone, always changing. And he said no one is noble or lowly by birth: 'by deeds one becomes an outcast; by deeds one becomes a brāhmin.' He let people of every caste, and eventually women, join his order — and he once turned a feared murderer into a monk.

He was called the Buddha — 'the one who woke up'. The word for that waking is bodhi, the word this site is named after. And the most useful part of his story may be the least mystical: he ran the experiment everyone respected, admitted it had failed, and changed course, even though it cost him every follower he had.

In early 1913, building a Ford Model T chassis took twelve hours and twenty-eight minutes. A team of workers walked the chassis through the Highland Park plant from station to station, gathering parts and fitting them as they went — skilled work, done well, and slow.

By that April, Ford's production engineers had already proven a faster method on a much smaller job: assembling a magneto flywheel, broken into twenty-nine separate operations performed by twenty-nine different workers, had cut assembly time from twenty minutes to five. The team extended the same logic to the whole chassis. Workers stayed in fixed positions; the chassis itself moved past them, first pulled by a rope and windlass, parts arriving in the exact sequence and quantity each station needed. Assembly time fell to five hours and fifty minutes. Then, once the conveyor's speed itself became a dial someone could simply turn up, it fell again — to ninety-three minutes.

The car hadn't changed. The line had. And the difference between twelve hours and ninety-three minutes was, in a very literal sense, the difference between a Model T only the wealthy could afford and a Model T fifteen million people eventually owned.

It came at a cost the price tag never showed. The same monotony that made a chassis in ninety-three minutes sent Highland Park's annual worker turnover past 300 percent — few people wanted to stand in one place repeating one motion for ten hours a day. Ford's fix, announced in January 1914, was blunt: pay roughly double the going wage, and let the line's speed, not the worker's judgment, do the rest of the work.

That tradeoff — extraordinary efficiency purchased at the price of the worker's own control over their pace — is the thread running through everything else on this page.

Verghese Kurien did not want the job in Anand. He had a mechanical engineering degree from Michigan State, a government bond obligating him to a posting somewhere in India, and no particular interest in dairy. What he found there in 1949 was a two-year-old cooperative with two village societies and a chairman, Tribhuvandas Patel, who talked him into staying.

What Kurien built from that starting point wasn't really a dairy company. It was a direction of ownership. Every private company in the world funnels control upward: a parent firm owns its subsidiaries, and authority runs down from whoever sits at the top. Kurien built the Anand Pattern to run the opposite way — farmers owning the village society, village societies owning the district union, district unions owning the state federation that put Amul on shelves nationally. Nobody above a tier could out-vote the tier that had elected them.

It worked for over fifty years, at a scale that turned a milk-importing country into the world's largest milk producer. In 2006, at 84, Kurien resigned the chairmanship of the federation he'd led for thirty-three years, after a disagreement with the very institution his own governance model had put in charge.

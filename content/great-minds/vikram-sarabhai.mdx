In the early 1960s, India could not reliably feed itself. Grain shipments were still arriving from abroad. Most villages went dark after sunset, and radio — let alone television — reached only a fraction of the country.

Into this country, Vikram Sarabhai proposed a satellite program.

Critics didn't call it ambitious. They called it absurd — a nation that couldn't guarantee its next harvest, reaching for orbit. Sarabhai's answer was never to defend space as a symbol of national pride. It was to explain, patiently and repeatedly, that the two problems were the same problem: a satellite could reach a village a road might take a generation to reach, and teach a classroom something electricity itself hadn't reached yet.

He wasn't asking whether India could afford the future. He was asking whether the future could finally reach the part of India that progress kept skipping.

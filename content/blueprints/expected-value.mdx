**Core question:** how do you compare two uncertain bets on the same scale, when one has a bigger prize and the other has better odds?

## The metaphor: the fairground ring-toss

A fairground ring-toss stall lines up several games, each with a different prize and a different chance of actually winning it. Most people walk toward the shiniest prize without doing any more math than that. The better approach is to multiply what each prize is worth by how likely you actually are to win it — that number is what the stall is really worth to you, and it often isn't the one with the biggest prize on the shelf.

<Callout type="insight" title="Probability × payoff, not payoff alone">
Expected value is the probability of each outcome multiplied by its payoff, summed across every possible outcome. It isn't a prediction of what happens this one time — it's the average result if you took the same bet many times over.
</Callout>

## Why the safer-looking choice can have a worse expected value

A stall with a modest prize and a 60% chance of winning it can have a higher expected value than a stall with a huge prize and only a 5% chance — once you actually do the multiplication — even though the huge prize is what catches your eye first. The big number pulls attention; the actual math often points somewhere less exciting.

<Callout type="question">
Before choosing based on how exciting a payoff feels, ask: what's the actual number — probability times payoff — for every option on the table, not just the one I already want to pick?
</Callout>

## Why this matters

Expected value reframes a decision away from "which outcome would feel best" and toward "which bet is actually worth taking" — a different, and usually more useful, question to be answering before you commit to anything uncertain.

**Core question:** how does a team know a story is actually finished, not just "coded"?

## The metaphor: the chef's ticket isn't done when food leaves the pan

A ticket in a kitchen isn't complete the moment food comes off the stove — it still has to pass plating, a temperature check, and reach the right table before the kitchen calls it done. "Off the stove" and "done" are different moments, and the whole kitchen agrees on exactly where the second one is, ticket after ticket, cook after cook. A software story works the same way: code complete is one moment, and done — tested, reviewed, documented, actually deployable — is a later one, and a team that hasn't agreed where that line sits will draw it in a different place every time.

<Callout type="insight" title="Not the same thing as acceptance criteria">
Acceptance criteria describe what "correct" looks like for one specific story — they change story to story. Definition of Done is the standing checklist every story has to clear regardless of what it's about: the same bar, applied every time, no matter which story is passing through it.
</Callout>

## Why teams that skip this fight the same fight every sprint

Without an explicit, shared line, "done" quietly means something different depending on who you ask. A developer means the code compiles and runs. QA means it's been tested. A product manager means it's actually live for users. None of them is wrong — they're each answering with their own default definition, because nobody wrote down the shared one. That gap doesn't show up as a single dramatic failure; it shows up as the same low-grade argument, sprint after sprint, about whether something is really finished.

<Callout type="question">
Before calling anything done, ask: would every role on this team point at the same moment in the process and agree that's where "done" happened?
</Callout>

## Why this matters

A written Definition of Done is what prevents "it's done" from meaning five different things depending on who says it. It costs a team one conversation to agree on, up front — which is far cheaper than renegotiating the same disagreement on every story that comes after it.

**Core question:** why do people take bigger risks once someone else is on the hook for the downside?

## The metaphor: your own car vs. a rental with full insurance

Same driver, same road, same skill — but the driving changes. In your own car, a scratch costs you money and you drive to avoid it. In a rental with full insurance, that same scratch costs you nothing, so you brake a little later and park a little closer to the wall, often without even noticing you've changed how you drive. Nothing about your ability changed. Only who pays if something goes wrong did.

<Callout type="insight" title="It's the incentive, not the person">
Moral hazard isn't about people being reckless by nature. It's what happens to ordinarily careful people once the consequences of a risk are moved onto someone else. The behavior changes — not the person underneath it.
</Callout>

## Where this shows up beyond cars

Deposit insurance can make depositors stop checking whether their bank is actually solvent, because their money is protected either way. A bailout precedent can make an institution take on risk it would never have touched if it knew, going in, that it alone would bear the full loss. In both cases, nothing about the underlying risk changed — only who was left holding it if things went wrong, and that shift alone was enough to change the behavior.

<Callout type="question">
Before assuming a safety net changes nothing about behavior, ask: who bears the cost if this goes wrong — and does the person making the risky decision know it won't be them?
</Callout>

## Why this matters

This is why insurers write deductibles into every policy instead of covering losses in full, why regulators impose capital requirements on banks instead of trusting caution alone, and why "who eats the loss" is one of the first questions worth asking about any system built to protect people from risk.

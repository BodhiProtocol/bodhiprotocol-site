**Core question:** why is there always a gap between what you can sell an asset for and what you can buy it for, right now, at the same moment?

## The metaphor: the airport currency counter

Every currency counter at an airport posts two numbers for the same currency: one rate if you're buying it, a worse one if you're selling it back. That gap isn't the counter's opinion about where the currency is really headed — it's what the counter charges for being ready to trade with you instantly, in either direction, without knowing in advance which one you'd want. A liquid stock market works the same way: the bid is the best price someone will pay you right now, the ask is the best price someone will sell to you right now, and the difference is the cost of that instant availability.

<Callout type="insight" title="The spread is a price, not a prediction">
The bid-ask spread isn't the market telling you where an asset is heading. It's compensation for whoever is quoting both sides — for standing ready to trade immediately and for the risk of being wrong before they can offload the position they just took on.
</Callout>

## What makes a spread wide or narrow

More buyers and sellers actively competing to quote a price narrows the spread — nobody wants to be the one quote that's obviously worse than everyone else's, so competition squeezes the gap down. More uncertainty about where the price is heading next widens it, because whoever is quoting both sides is taking on more risk of getting caught holding a position just before the price moves against them. That's why a heavily traded blue-chip stock has a razor-thin spread, and a thinly traded small-cap can have one wide enough to notice.

<Callout type="question">
Before treating a quoted price as "the" price, check: which side of the spread does it apply to — and is what I'm about to do a purchase or a sale?
</Callout>

## Why this matters

On a liquid, heavily traded asset, the spread is small enough to ignore most of the time. On an illiquid or volatile one, it's a real cost, paid instantly at the moment of the trade, before the market has even had a chance to move in your favor or against you — which makes it one of the first things worth checking before trading anything thinly traded.

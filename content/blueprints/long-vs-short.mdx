**Core question:** what does it actually mean to bet that a price will fall, not just that it will rise?

## The metaphor: borrowing the neighbor's lawnmower

Buying an asset and hoping it rises is the ordinary way almost everyone already trades — you own it, you wait, you hope it's worth more later. Shorting reverses every step. It's like borrowing your neighbor's lawnmower, selling it today for cash, and promising to hand back an equivalent lawnmower later. You're betting mowers get cheaper before you have to go buy one back to return — borrow the asset, sell it now, buy it back later, hand back what you borrowed, and pocket the difference if the price fell in between.

<Callout type="insight" title="Same move, opposite outcome">
The exact same price move helps one position and hurts the other. A long position profits when the price rises and loses when it falls. A short position profits when the price falls and loses when it rises — it's not a different bet on a different asset, it's the mirror image of the same one.
</Callout>

## The asymmetry that makes shorting riskier

A long position's downside is capped: the asset can only fall to zero, so the most you can ever lose is what you paid for it. A short position's downside has no such ceiling — there's no upper limit on how high the price you eventually have to buy back at could go, which means the loss on a short, in principle, isn't capped at all. That asymmetry is exactly why margin requirements exist specifically for short positions, and rarely bite the same way on a simple long one.

<Callout type="question">
Before shorting anything, ask: what's my maximum possible loss if I'm completely wrong — and is there actually a ceiling on it?
</Callout>

## Why this matters

The mechanics of a short — borrow, sell, buy back, return — are just the plumbing. The asymmetry in how much you can lose is the part that actually changes how a short position should be sized, monitored, and hedged compared to an equivalent long one.

It's 41 degrees outside. You walk into the small shop at the end of your lane, the one with the faded awning and the shopkeeper who knows your father's name. You hand over one ₹10 note and get back a cold, fizzy, slightly salty bottle of Lahori Zeera. Done in four seconds.

That note is about to go on a journey. It'll be split, taxed, shared, trucked, and chipped away at, and by the end, the company whose name is printed on that bottle will keep about **25 paise** of it. A quarter of a rupee. The shopkeeper who handed it to you keeps roughly five times as much.

That sounds like a company losing. It's actually the reason a drink invented in a Punjab kitchen in 2016 now sits in more than 5 lakh shops across India, going head to head with Coca-Cola and Pepsi. Let's follow the note.

*(A quick word on the numbers: the tax is exact, the company's figures are from its reported FY25 results, and the per-bottle split is our estimate built from those. [How we built it](#how-we-estimated-the-split) is at the end.)*

## Stop 1: A Price That Never Moves

Think of the ₹10 note as a key. Everyone in India has one. Nobody has to break it, count change, or think twice about spending it. A price that matches a single note or coin isn't really a price at all. It's a shortcut past the part of your brain that asks "is this worth it?"

Businesses have known this for more than a century. In the United States, a bottle of Coca-Cola cost 5 cents from 1886 until 1959, roughly seventy years without a price change. Vending machines took a single nickel, and changing the price would have meant changing the machines and the habit at the same time. More recently, AriZona Iced Tea has kept "99¢" printed on its big 23-ounce cans since 1992, absorbing cost increases rather than breaking a number its customers had memorised.

Lahori made the same bet in India. It launched at ₹10 and has kept its core bottle at ₹10 ever since, with co-founder Saurabh Munjal describing ₹10 as a particularly important price point for reaching India's mass market. For a curious ten-year-old, it's a treat you can buy with your own pocket money. For a labourer on a construction site, it's the cheapest cold thing with flavour in the shop. For a business analyst, it's something sharper: a **fixed price point**, a constraint the whole rest of the business has to be designed around. Every cost you're about to see has to fit inside that one note, forever. The price can't flex, so everything else has to.

## Stop 2: The Fizz Tax

Before anyone in the drinks business sees a paisa, the government takes its share.

Think of it like a toll booth right at the start of the road. It doesn't matter who's driving or how far they're going. Everyone pays the same cut first.

Governments around the world tax sugary, fizzy drinks harder than ordinary food, partly for revenue and partly to nudge people away from sugar. The United Kingdom introduced its Soft Drinks Industry Levy in 2018, charging manufacturers by how much sugar each drink contains. In 2017, Philadelphia put a tax of 1.5 cents per ounce on sweetened drinks, which works out to more than a dollar on a two-litre bottle.

India's version is bigger and simpler. Since 22 September 2025, under the reworked GST structure, carbonated and sweetened drinks sit in the top **40%** slab. Before that, the combined rate was also 40%: 28% GST plus a 12% compensation cess. And the detail that matters for Lahori is the fizz itself. Under the same rules, many *still* fruit-juice drinks sit at just 5%. Add carbon dioxide and a drink jumps into the 40% slab.

For a ₹10 bottle, the maths works like this: ₹10 includes the tax, so the pre-tax price is ₹10 ÷ 1.40 = ₹7.14, and the tax is the remaining **₹2.86**. The tax is collected at every step of the chain, with each business claiming back what it already paid, but the total that ends up with the government is the same ₹2.86 per bottle.

**Left in the note: ₹7.14.**

## Stop 3: The Shopkeeper

Here's the stop that explains everything.

Think of a school canteen with two brands of juice on the counter. You walk up, you're thirsty, you don't have a favourite. The person behind the counter says "try this one, it's good." You take it. Now imagine one brand pays the canteen more for every bottle sold. Which one do you think gets recommended?

In a small Indian shop, you don't walk the aisles. There are no aisles. You stand at the counter and ask for "something cold," and the shopkeeper decides what goes into your hand. The person who actually chooses the drink is often **the shopkeeper**, not you.

The global giants understand this too, in their own way. In Mexico and across Latin America, Coca-Cola's bottlers are well known for placing branded coolers in tiny neighbourhood shops, the *tienditas*. The fridge is valuable to the shopkeeper, and whatever sits inside it, cold and at eye level, is what sells. Coca-Cola wins the shop by owning the fridge.

Lahori didn't have the money to put fridges in lakhs of shops. So it did the opposite, and paid the shopkeeper directly. It was reported to offer retailers **1.5x to 2x the margin** global brands paid on comparable packs, and, crucially, no dependency on refrigeration. A jeera drink with salt and spice is still drinkable at room temperature in a way a cola isn't, so the bottles can sit on a shelf and still sell.

Shopkeepers don't publish their margins, so this is where our estimate gets softer. A reasonable range is ₹1 to ₹1.50 for every ₹10 bottle sold. We use **₹1.20**. On a hot day in a busy shop selling a few crates, that adds up to real money for a small business owner, and it's why Lahori crossed more than 5 lakh retail touchpoints without a celebrity ambassador or a big advertising budget in its early years. More than 99% of its sales go through general trade: kirana stores, dhabas, railway stalls and village markets.

**Left in the note: ₹5.94.**

## Stop 4: The Distributor

Between the factory and the shop, someone has to hold the stock, lend the shopkeeper a little credit, and turn up every week with a van.

Think of the distributor as a wholesaler who buys in bulk from the factory, keeps the stock in a godown, and sells it in smaller lots to every shop on the route, taking a small slice for the trouble and the risk.

In the United States, big food companies often cut this layer out entirely. PepsiCo's Frito-Lay is the classic example of **direct store delivery**: its own route drivers deliver to stores and stock the shelves themselves. That gives the brand control over every shelf, but it's expensive, and it only works at huge scale.

Lahori went the other way and leaned on independent distributors, whose network reportedly grew to more than 2,000. And it did something unusual for an unknown brand: it asked them to pay in advance. Distributors pushed back at first, so the founders went shop by shop, got retailers to stock and sell it, and came back with proof that people were already buying. Once demand existed, Lahori could set the terms: advance payment, not credit. The distributor funds the stock sitting in the godown and carries the credit risk of lending to hundreds of small shops, so Lahori doesn't wait to get paid. In return, the distributor keeps a thin cut per bottle. We estimate **₹0.45**.

This is the last stop before the money reaches Lahori. Of your ₹10, the company now receives roughly ₹5.49. Everything from here on is Lahori spending its own money.

**Left in the note: ₹5.49.**

## Stop 5: The Truck

A ₹10 bottle of Lahori is almost entirely water. That's a problem, because water is heavy and cheap. You're paying to move something that weighs a lot but sells for very little.

Think of trying to earn money by delivering glasses of water across town on a bicycle. The water is nearly free. The cycling is the entire cost of the business.

This is exactly why Coca-Cola's global system is built the way it is. The Coca-Cola Company mostly doesn't ship finished drinks around the world. It sells concentrate, a small and valuable ingredient, to bottlers who add the water, sugar and fizz close to where the drinks will be sold. The heavy part never travels far.

Lahori started close to home, manufacturing in Rupnagar, Punjab, and selling across Punjab, Haryana, Delhi, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan and Himachal. Growing beyond the north meant moving bottles much further. In FY25, as it pushed into South and West India, logistics and transport costs **doubled to ₹52 crore**, about a tenth of what the company spent. Its answer mirrors Coca-Cola's: work with contract manufacturers in other regions and reportedly build a facility in Lucknow, so bottles are filled closer to the people drinking them.

On our per-bottle maths, freight takes about **₹0.53** of your ₹10.

**Left in the note: ₹4.96.**

## Stop 6: The Jeera, the Bottle, and the People

Now for the part you'd expect to be the whole story: actually making the drink.

Think of a home recipe scaled up a million times. The first version, made in co-founder Nikhil Doda's kitchen in 2016, started with cumin, black salt and lemon. The drink that went on sale adds black pepper, dry ginger and sendha namak, plus fizz. Every one of those, plus sugar, plus the PET or glass bottle, plus the cap and the label, has to be bought, over and over, at a scale that went from 96,000 bottles a day in the early years to about 12 lakh a day by 2022.

AriZona faced the same squeeze in the US. Aluminium, sugar and freight all got more expensive over the years, and its founders have repeatedly said they chose to absorb those costs rather than change the 99¢ on the can. When the price is the promise, rising costs don't get passed on. They come straight out of the company's share.

For Lahori, procurement (ingredients, bottles, packaging and bought-in stock) was the biggest cost by far: **₹316 crore in FY25**, up more than 70% in a year. Employee costs came to ₹40 crore, plus another ₹23 crore for contract workers brought in to handle peak summer production. Spread across one ₹10 bottle, that's roughly:

- **₹3.21** for the jeera, sugar, bottle and packaging
- **₹0.64** for the people who make, pack and sell it
- **₹0.86** for everything else: power, rent, marketing, contract bottling fees, depreciation, interest and income tax

**Left in the note: ₹0.25.**

## Stop 7: What Reaches Lahori

Twenty-five paise. That's what's left for the company after everyone else has been paid.

Think of it like a school fete stall that sells a lot of lemonade. It isn't making a fortune on each cup, but it's selling more cups than anyone else in the ground, and next year it gets the best spot by the gate.

Compare that with the company at the top of the global drinks business. Coca-Cola has earned more than 20 cents of net profit on every dollar of revenue in recent years, because its main product is concentrate and the brand, while bottlers carry the trucks, factories and shelf fights. Lahori has chosen the opposite position. It does the heavy, low-margin work itself and keeps a sliver.

The FY25 numbers show exactly that trade. Revenue jumped **73% to ₹540 crore**, from ₹312 crore. Profit stayed **flat at about ₹25 crore**, and EBITDA margin slipped to around 10%. On the surface that looks like a company growing without getting richer. Underneath, it's a company deliberately spending its extra money on reach (more states, more distributors, more freight, more summer workers) instead of banking it.

## The Verdict

So is 25 paise a failure? Only if the job of that ₹10 bottle were to make money. It isn't. Its job is to win the counter.

Investors seem to agree. In May 2025, Motilal Oswal invested ₹200 crore in the company for a 7.14% stake, a deal that valued Lahori at roughly **₹2,800 crore**. They aren't paying for the 25 paise. They're paying for the 5 lakh shopkeepers who now reach for a Lahori bottle when someone asks for something cold, and for what that habit could earn once the network is built and freight costs per bottle come down.

The risk sits on the same shelf. Reliance has brought back Campa Cola at aggressive prices, and the big global brands have more money to spend on shopkeeper margins than Lahori does. A business built on out-paying the shopkeeper is only safe as long as nobody out-pays you. And the 40% fizz tax and the fixed ₹10 price mean Lahori can't solve a squeeze by charging more. It can only win on volume and on cost.

That's the hidden economics in one line: **the customer pays ₹10, the government takes the first bite, the shopkeeper gets paid to choose, and the company lives on whatever's left.**

## How We Estimated the Split

For readers who want to check the maths, or argue with it:

- **Tax (₹2.86): exact.** A ₹10 MRP including 40% GST gives ₹10 ÷ 1.40 = ₹7.14 before tax.
- **Shopkeeper (₹1.20) and distributor (₹0.45): estimates.** Neither is published. The shopkeeper figure sits inside a ₹1 to ₹1.50 range consistent with the reported "1.5x to 2x" margin advantage. The distributor figure is a typical thin wholesale cut. Change either and the rest scales slightly.
- **Everything inside Lahori: FY25 ratios.** What reaches the company (₹5.49) is split using its reported FY25 cost structure on ₹540 crore of revenue: procurement ₹316 crore (58.5%), freight ₹52 crore (9.6%), people ₹63 crore (11.7%), profit ₹25 crore (4.6%), and everything else making up the remaining 15.6%.
- **What it assumes.** Company revenue includes larger packs and other flavours, not just the ₹10 bottle, so these are averages applied to a single bottle, not Lahori's internal cost sheet for that SKU.
- **A sanity check.** ₹540 crore ÷ ₹5.49 per bottle works out to roughly 98 crore ₹10-bottle equivalents a year, or about 27 lakh a day. That's in the same range as the roughly 20 lakh bottles a day the company was later reported to be producing (with larger packs counting for more than one ₹10 equivalent), which suggests the estimate isn't far off.

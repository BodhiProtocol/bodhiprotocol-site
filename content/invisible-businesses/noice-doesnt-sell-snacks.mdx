Order a packet of khakhra or a bottle of cold-pressed juice through Swiggy Instamart today and a brand called Noice might be sitting at the top of the results. It's tempting to read that as a familiar retail trick — a cheaper in-house alternative parked next to the branded product, the same move Amazon runs with AmazonBasics. That comparison undersells what's actually happening, because Noice isn't just stocked by Swiggy. It's ranked by Swiggy, delivered by Swiggy, and priced by Swiggy — three separate contests any other brand on the same app has to win, buy into, or negotiate, one at a time.

## The Invisible Business

Every brand competing on Instamart that isn't Noice is fighting three battles independently. It has to win the search-ranking algorithm to show up for the right query. It has to pay for promotional slot inventory to get featured placement over a rival. And it has to hit whatever price and margin the platform is willing to list at all, or risk losing the listing entirely. Instamart, as the operator, controls every one of those levers before a single Noice product is even made.

That's not a hypothetical advantage. Trade coverage of the quick-commerce sector has described Instamart's private-label push explicitly as a way to directly control pricing, assortment, and app listing for its own products — the same three things an outside brand has to fight the algorithm, buy shelf space, or cut margin to get. A khakhra maker with a bigger factory and a bigger ad budget than Noice's forty-kitchen network still has to win Instamart's search results from outside the walls. Noice doesn't compete for that placement. It starts there.

Since its August 2025 launch, it has shipped more than 200 products across 13 categories — artisanal breads, cold-pressed juices, sodas, paneer, biscuits, sweets, fresh batters, traditional regional snacks — sourced not from one large contract manufacturer but from over 40 small kitchens and makers spread across cities like Calicut, Mangalore, and Sonipat. Every one of those SKUs enters the app already ranked, already featured, already priced by the same company deciding whether a competitor's product gets to exist on the page at all.

## Why It Isn't Just Another Private Label

The old private-label playbook sells on price: a plainer package, a lower number, a quiet bet that shoppers will trade brand trust for a few rupees saved. Noice does the opposite. Its packaging borrows from Indian truck art — bold colors, maximalist illustration — deliberately built to look like a brand someone discovered, not a discount someone settled for. Its marketing leans into its own name sounding like "noise" instead of hiding it. Nothing about it is styled to read as "Swiggy's cheaper option."

That positioning isn't just aesthetic — it's the entire economic logic. India's quick-commerce apps have found that private labels can carry 30 to 40% higher gross margins than stocking someone else's branded product on the same shelf, and that gap only holds if shoppers don't perceive the private label as a downgrade. By competing on discovery and provenance instead of price, Noice keeps that margin advantage while still charging full, sometimes premium, price — something a generic store brand copying a bestseller could never do.

Which means Instamart isn't only deciding what to stock anymore. It's deciding what to make — and unlike a Britannia or a Haldiram's negotiating for shelf space from outside, it never has to ask its own algorithm for permission to be seen.

Seen this way, Noice was never really competing for space in the snack aisle. It was Swiggy noticing that the three things every other brand has to pay for — the ranking, the placement, the right to be listed at all — were already sitting inside its own app, unbilled, the moment it decided to become a brand instead of just a landlord.

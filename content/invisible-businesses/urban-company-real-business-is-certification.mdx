Book a plumber through Urban Company and someone shows up wearing a branded uniform, carrying a company-issued toolkit, and running through the same diagnostic checklist a plumber three cities away would run. It reads like a small thing — a nice-to-have polish on top of a service marketplace. It isn't. That consistency is the product being sold, and it exists because the person standing at the door survived something most customers never see: an applicant funnel that rejects roughly seven out of every ten people who try to get in.

## The Invisible Business

Most services marketplaces are matchmakers. They post a listing, take a cut, and step back — the vetting, if it happens at all, is the customer's problem. Urban Company runs the opposite model. Every professional on the platform passes through government ID verification, police checks, in-person interviews, skill assessments, and reference checks before they're allowed to accept a single booking. Only about 25 to 30% of applicants clear that funnel. The rest are turned away before they ever reach a customer's door.

The professionals who do make it aren't done. Urban Company has put roughly ₹72 crore into training and skilling infrastructure, running more than 100 dedicated training centres in partnership with the National Skill Development Corporation to standardize instruction and issue digital certification across categories — salon and spa, electrical work, appliance repair, plumbing, carpentry, pest control. A new professional doesn't just get accepted; they get built, in a company classroom, to a company specification, before they're certified to carry the company's name into someone's living room.

That's the part a five-star rating quietly stands in for. When a customer books a service, they're not evaluating an individual — they're trusting a credential Urban Company controls the issuance of, end to end.

## Why The Commission Is The Smaller Business

The obvious revenue line is commission: Urban Company takes an estimated 25 to 30% of each professional's service fee, plus flat lead-generation charges reported on higher-ticket categories like renovation and appliance installation. That alone would make it a normal, if profitable, marketplace.

What makes the certification funnel a business in its own right is everything layered on top of it. New applicants pay a training and assessment fee to enter the pipeline. Once certified, professionals buy branded consumables and toolkits from Urban Company at negotiated group rates — not because a rule forces them to, but because the branded kit is part of what makes them recognizably "Urban Company" rather than an independent tradesperson who happens to have downloaded an app. Every one of those purchases is revenue the company wouldn't collect if it were simply listing independent professionals and taking a cut.

Run that forward across more than 30,000 trained professionals and the funnel stops looking like an onboarding cost and starts looking like the actual business: a company that manufactures the professional, certifies the professional, equips the professional, and only then lets a customer pay for an hour of their time. The plumbing call is the visible transaction. The pipeline that produced the plumber is the one nobody's billing statement itemizes — and the one Urban Company would be a very different, much smaller company without.

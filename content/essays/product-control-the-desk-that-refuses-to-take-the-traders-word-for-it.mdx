In the first days of 2008, two senior bankers at Credit Suisse got on a recorded phone line and admitted to each other that the prices they had just put on their books were too high. Then they said the thing that gave them away: they were worried someone in the bank would *spot* it.

Not that the market would turn. Not that a trade would go wrong. That a colleague — one specific kind of colleague — would look at their numbers and see through them.

To see who they were afraid of, start somewhere much smaller. Think of a kid at school with a rare trading card. Ask him what it's worth and he'll say five hundred rupees, easily. But the last three kids who actually sold that card in the playground got two hundred. So what's it worth? Two hundred — that's what someone actually paid. His five hundred is what he *hopes*. And if his pocket money depends on how rich his collection looks, he has every reason to keep saying five hundred.

Every trading desk has that kid, every evening. Someone has to say what the desk's positions are worth, and so how much it made today. The trader knows those positions best — and has the most riding on the answer. **Product Control** exists because of those two facts together. It's the friend who walks over to the playground and checks what cards are really selling for. It's who those two bankers were afraid of.

## What Product Control actually does

Product Control sits in the middle office and reports to the Chief Financial Officer — never to the head of trading. That reporting line is the whole foundation. It owns three jobs.

**1. Rebuild the day's profit.** Each desk sends its own evening estimate — the *flash* P&L. Product Control builds the *official* P&L for the bank's books from its own trade records and end-of-day prices. If the two don't roughly match, someone answers for the gap by morning.

**2. Explain it.** Think of your household budget. Your balance fell ₹8,000 this month. You want to know *why*: ₹5,000 more on groceries, ₹2,000 for a doctor, and ₹1,000 you can't account for. That last ₹1,000 is the one that should worry you. A **P&L explain** (or attribution) does the same to a trading book, splitting the day's result into its causes. For an options desk it might look like this:

| Source of today's P&L | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|
| Market moved (delta, gamma) | +38 |
| Volatility changed (vega) | −12 |
| One day passed (theta) | −6 |
| New trades booked today | +14 |
| Fees and funding | −3 |
| **Explained** | **+31** |
| **Reported P&L** | **+53** |
| **Unexplained** | **+22** |

Every explained line has a cause — [delta](/essays/delta-the-gear-ratio-between-an-option-and-its-stock) and [gamma](/essays/gamma-the-feedback-loop-hiding-inside-every-delta-hedge) against the market's move, [vega](/essays/vega-the-price-of-not-knowing-what-happens-next) against volatility, [theta](/essays/theta-the-cost-of-being-right-too-late) for the day gone by. The bottom line has none. ₹22 lakh of profit that no market movement accounts for is one of the loudest alarms in a bank, because a profit nobody can explain is almost always one of two things: a broken model, or a price somebody chose.

**3. Check the prices.** This is the playground walk, formally **Independent Price Verification (IPV)**. Traders mark their own positions daily; Product Control compares those marks — at least monthly, far more often for liquid books — against sources the trader doesn't control: exchange closes, broker quotes, consensus pricing services, recent real trades. A mark outside tolerance gets challenged; if the trader can't defend it, it moves, and the P&L moves with it.

How hard that check is depends on how much real market exists for the thing being priced. Accounting standards — IFRS 13 internationally, Ind AS 113 in India — sort every position into three levels. Think of a cricket bat:

- **Level 1** — the exact bat is in a shop window with a price tag. A listed share, a benchmark government bond. The price is observed.
- **Level 2** — that bat isn't for sale, but a nearly identical one is, so you start from its price and adjust. A corporate bond valued off similar bonds; most standard derivatives.
- **Level 3** — a custom bat nobody has ever sold. There's no price to observe, only a model built on assumptions somebody chose. Complex structured products, illiquid loans, distressed debt.

Level 1 is nearly impossible to fake. Level 3 is where Product Control earns its salary — and because even an honest Level 3 value is an estimate, banks hold back **valuation reserves** (prudent valuation adjustments): a cushion against bid-offer spreads, model uncertainty and positions too big to sell at the screen price. The kid saying "₹2,000 — but I'd only bet on ₹1,600 if I had to sell tomorrow."

This is a different check from its neighbours. [Reconciliation](/essays/reconciliation-the-discipline-of-assuming-two-records-already-disagree) confirms two *records* of a trade agree — and passes happily if both carry the same wrong price. [The risk engine](/essays/risk-management-engines-the-gauge-the-driver-isnt-supposed-to-touch) asks what the book *could* lose tomorrow. [Rogue trading](/essays/rogue-trading-when-the-person-trading-is-also-the-person-checking-the-trade) is about trades hidden from the bank entirely. Product Control's failure is quieter: every trade is on the books in plain sight. The lie is the price written next to it.

## Example 1: Credit Suisse picked the profit first and the prices second

By late 2007, subprime mortgage bonds were collapsing, and Credit Suisse's structured credit group held billions of dollars of them. According to the SEC's 2012 complaint, the group's global head, Kareem Serageldin, regularly told his head of hedge trading, David Higgs, what P&L he wanted to see — and Higgs directed two traders to mark the book to produce it. The SEC's phrase for this is the cleanest definition of a product control failure ever put in writing: **"marking to P&L" rather than marking to fair value.** The answer came first. The prices were worked backwards from it.

The SEC alleged the group deliberately ignored specific market information showing sharp declines, and that the scheme peaked at year-end 2007, with roughly $3 billion of subprime bonds carried at overstated prices — in time for bonus season and, for Serageldin, a hoped-for promotion. That's when the recorded call happened. The people being checked knew exactly what the check was, and were counting on getting past it.

For a while, they did. When the mispricing was caught in February 2008, Credit Suisse disclosed $2.65 billion in additional subprime-related losses. Britain's regulator, the FSA, later fined the bank £5.6 million, finding it hadn't adequately supervised the group, hadn't acted in time on concerns already raised about its pricing, and lacked the systems and controls to recognise — for around five months — that those positions were wrongly valued. Serageldin pleaded guilty and was sentenced in November 2013 to 30 months in prison. He is widely described as the only Wall Street banker jailed in the United States for conduct tied to the 2008 financial crisis — and his crime wasn't a bad bet. It was a false price.

Why five months? Subprime bonds in a frozen market were Level 3 in all but name — almost no real trades to check against, which made the traders genuinely the best-informed people in the building. That's the trap Product Control is built for: the less observable a price, the more a controller must lean on the trader's judgement — precisely when that judgement can't be taken on trust.

## Example 2: India took the price out of the bank's hands

Indian bank treasuries are dominated not by exotic structured credit but by huge holdings of government securities — liquid, standardized, mostly Level 1 or 2. So the danger isn't one trader inventing a price. It's a whole bank, facing a whole bond market falling at once, wanting a softer number than the market is giving. India's answer has been to do Product Control's job at the level of the system, taking the choice away at three layers.

**The price.** Since March 31, 2018, Indian banks have valued government securities using prices published by Financial Benchmarks India Ltd (FBIL), which took over the job from the industry body FIMMDA. The bank doesn't produce a mark and have it checked; the mark arrives from outside, identical for every bank. It's IPV built into the market's plumbing. (RBI has long required bank treasuries to separate front, middle and back office, so valuation doesn't sit with the dealers either.)

**The model price.** RBI's Master Direction on investment portfolios, issued September 12, 2023 and in force from April 1, 2024, brought the Level 1/2/3 hierarchy into Indian bank rules — then drew a hard line under the bottom level. Net unrealised gains on Level 3 investments can't be paid out as dividends, and are deducted from the bank's core CET1 capital. A bank may *report* profit from a price nobody can observe; it may not *count* it. The kid's parents saying: "Fine, tell us the card's worth five hundred. Your pocket money is still based on two hundred."

**The timing.** A mark is only honest if the loss shows up when it happens. In 2018, after Indian bond yields jumped 67 basis points in a single quarter, RBI let banks spread the mark-to-market losses for the December 2017 and March 2018 quarters over up to four quarters, citing the systemic impact — and paired that relief with a new Investment Fluctuation Reserve so banks would be cushioned next time. The next time came in 2026. After the West Asia crisis began in late February, the 10-year government bond yield pushed past 7% and treasury books took the hit. Banks asked to stagger the losses again. This time, according to reporting on April 15, 2026, RBI refused: the full impact had to land in the March 2026 results.

2018 next to 2026 is the argument a product controller has with a desk every month — held between a regulator and an entire banking system. The loss is real today. Spreading it over a year doesn't make it smaller, only harder to see.

## Why this matters for a Business Analyst

### Back to the playground

"Build a P&L report" isn't a requirement. Credit Suisse had a P&L report every single day. What it lacked was a system that made the number hard to choose. A BA scoping anything that touches valuation or P&L should push until the requirement answers these:

- **Where does each price come from, and who can override it?** Every mark carries its source (exchange, FBIL, vendor, broker, model) and a timestamp. Any manual override needs a reason code and a second person's approval — [maker-checker](/essays/rogue-trading-when-the-person-trading-is-also-the-person-checking-the-trade), applied to prices instead of trades.
- **What's the tolerance, and what happens on a breach?** "IPV flags differences" is unfinished. The requirement is the threshold by product and fair value level, who gets the exception, how long they have, and whether the book can be signed off with it open.
- **How stale is too stale?** A bond price that hasn't moved in ten trading days while its peers are repricing is a Level 3 mark in disguise. The system should catch it, not wait for someone to notice.
- **Is unexplained P&L a number or a gate?** If ₹22 lakh unexplained doesn't stop the official P&L being published, it's just a report. For QA, this is the test case that matters most: inject an out-of-tolerance mark and a P&L move the attribution can't explain, and prove the sign-off stops.
- **Does every position carry its fair value level?** The level drives reserves, the CET1 deduction under RBI's rules, and disclosures. A Level 3 position quietly tagged Level 2 escapes all three.

Every one of those rests on the same principle: the person whose bonus depends on a number should never be the last person to touch it.

## The hidden tradeoff

Product Control is structurally outgunned. Traders are paid more, know the instruments better, and are usually right about their own marks. A controller who fights every mark makes enemies; one who stops becomes the rubber stamp Credit Suisse's bankers were counting on. And the positions where the controller must lean hardest on the trader are exactly where a false mark is easiest to hide. That tension never resolves. The best a bank can do is make pushing back cheap — prices sourced automatically, tolerances enforced by the system, overrides that leave a trail someone else will read.

## Lighthouse Insight

The kid with the trading card isn't necessarily lying — maybe he truly believes his card is special. That's the point: Product Control was never built to catch liars. It's built so that belief, however sincere, is never enough to put a number on the books. Two bankers at Credit Suisse wrote down the profit they wanted, worked out the prices afterwards, then spent a phone call worrying about the one colleague whose job was to walk over to the playground and check. India built that walk into the plumbing. Either way the rule is the same: what you own is worth what someone would actually pay for it today — and nobody gets to answer that question for themselves.

Sit in enough project meetings and you'll hear all three titles thrown at the same person in the same hour — "can the BA confirm," "check with the PM," "that's really a PO call" — as if they're interchangeable labels for whoever happens to be running the show. They aren't. Each title exists because it answers a question the other two can't: what should we build, what does that mean exactly, and when do we get it. Collapse those three questions into one job and the collapse doesn't make the work simpler — it just means whichever question gets skipped shows up later as a production incident.

## Think of it as renovating a house

A homeowner decides they want a bigger kitchen. That's it — that's the whole instruction. An architect turns "bigger kitchen" into an actual drawing: exact dimensions, where the plumbing has to move, which wall is load-bearing and can't come down. A general contractor takes that drawing and turns it into a schedule: which trade shows up which week, what it costs, what happens if the plumber is late. Three different people, three different skills, and none of them can quietly do the other two's job without something breaking — the homeowner can't draw the plumbing route from instinct, the architect can't promise a move-in date, and the contractor can't decide whether the kitchen should even get bigger in the first place.

That's the same split that sits underneath Product Owner, Business Analyst, and Project Manager. The Product Owner is the homeowner — they decide what gets built and why it matters. The Business Analyst is the architect — they turn that decision into something precise enough to actually build. The Project Manager is the contractor — they turn the precise version into a schedule, a budget, and a delivery date.

## The Product Owner decides what, and why it's worth doing now

A Product Owner's real job is prioritization, not description. Given a backlog of twenty things a bank's trading desk could build next quarter — a real-time trade-breaks dashboard, a faster FX netting workflow, a self-service report for compliance — the PO's job is to decide which one earns the next sprint, based on business value, regulatory pressure, or client demand, and to be able to defend that ranking to a steering committee. A PO who can't say *why* something is priority one over priority four isn't doing the job; "the client asked for it" isn't a reason on its own until it's weighed against everything else waiting in that same backlog.

What a PO is *not* responsible for is precision. "Build a faster FX netting workflow" is a perfectly good backlog item and a completely unusable requirement — it doesn't say what "faster" means, which currency pairs, what happens when two trades net to zero, or who has to approve an exception. That gap is deliberate, not a failure of the PO's writing. It's the next job's entire reason to exist.

## The Business Analyst decides exactly what that means

This is the architect's drawing. A BA takes "faster FX netting workflow" and turns it into something an engineer and a QA tester can both read the same way: the exact trigger, the exact output, and — the part that actually takes the skill — every edge case the one-line version left out. What happens if a trade fails validation mid-netting? Does a partial net get reversed or held? Who's notified? [Writing Requirements That Survive Contact With Engineering](/essays/writing-requirements-that-survive-contact-with-engineering) covers the mechanics of getting from a vague sentence to something testable; [From Stakeholder Sentence to Acceptance Criteria](/essays/from-stakeholder-sentence-to-acceptance-criteria) covers the same gap from the elicitation side.

The BA doesn't decide whether the netting workflow ships this quarter or next — that was the PO's call, made before the BA got involved. And the BA doesn't commit to a delivery date — that's the next job. What the BA owns is whether, once built, the thing actually does what the business meant, in every case, not just the obvious one.

## The Project Manager decides when, at what cost, and around what risk

The contractor's schedule. A PM takes the BA's precise requirement and works out the sequencing: which team builds it, what it depends on, what could go wrong, and what date goes on the steering committee slide. That's a real skill with its own failure mode — a PM who commits a delivery date before the requirement is fully scoped is promising a number nobody has actually earned yet. [Scope Creep Is a Timing Problem](/essays/scope-creep-is-a-timing-problem) is really this exact failure from a different angle: a date gets fixed early, the requirement keeps evolving after, and the PM ends up defending a commitment the BA hadn't finished writing when it was made.

A PM also isn't the person who should be deciding what "faster" means in the netting workflow, or whether the workflow was the right priority at all. When a PM starts answering those questions instead of routing them, the project doesn't move faster — it just means those decisions get made by whoever's in the room with a calendar open, instead of by whoever actually owns them.

## Where the three collide

The failure isn't usually one person doing a bad job — it's the three questions getting answered out of order, in the same meeting, by whoever speaks next. A stakeholder raises a request. Someone with "PM" in their title, eager to look responsive, commits a delivery date on the spot. Now the BA is scoping a requirement backward from a date that was set before anyone knew what the requirement actually involved, and if the true scope doesn't fit the promised date, "faster" quietly gets redefined to whatever *can* ship by then — not what the business actually needed. The PO, who was supposed to be the one deciding if this even beat the other nineteen things in the backlog, never got asked.

None of this requires three separate people. Plenty of banking teams run lean enough that one person carries BA and PM together, sometimes all three. That's not automatically wrong — it's wrong when the person forgets which hat they're wearing mid-sentence, answers a scheduling question with prioritization logic, or answers a prioritization question with a schedule, because nobody in the room, including them, paused to notice which of the three questions was actually on the table. [What Nobody Tells a New Business Analyst](/essays/what-nobody-tells-a-new-business-analyst) covers a version of this same problem — a new BA taking a request at face value without checking who actually owns the decision behind it.

## The one-question check

Before answering anything in a scoping meeting, it's worth silently sorting the question that was just asked into one of three buckets: **is this asking what we should build, what that means exactly, or when and at what cost?** Answer a scheduling question with a prioritization argument, or a precision question with a date, and the mismatch won't show up in that meeting — it'll show up three sprints later, as a requirement that was scoped to fit a date instead of the other way around.

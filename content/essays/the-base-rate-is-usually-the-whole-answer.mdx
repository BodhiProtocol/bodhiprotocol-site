Your phone buzzes. It's the clinic. Your test came back positive.

Before you feel anything else, here's a number worth knowing: for a disease that affects 1 in 1,000 people, even a test that's right 99% of the time still means you're more likely to be fine than sick. That's not a typo. It's called a base rate — and almost nobody reaches for one when it actually matters.

## Do the math

Imagine 100,000 people take that test. About 100 of them actually have the disease, and the test correctly catches 99 of those. But among the 99,900 healthy people, 1% still test positive by mistake — another 999 people. Add it up: 1,098 positive results, and only 99 of them are real. A positive test means roughly a 9% chance of actually being sick. Not 99%. Nine.

The test didn't get worse. The disease was just rare to begin with — and rarity doesn't disappear just because a confident result showed up.

## The loud story beats the quiet average

This isn't a medical quirk — it's how evidence gets misread everywhere. A product with 4,000 five-star reviews gets one scathing one-star review, and that single story sticks harder than the other 3,999 combined, even though the base rate says the product is excellent. The vivid case is loud. The base rate is quiet. Quiet loses, unless you go looking for it on purpose.

## Combine, don't replace

The fix isn't to ignore the vivid evidence. It's to start from the base rate and adjust, instead of throwing the base rate out and letting the new evidence decide alone. When something is rare, even strong evidence isn't strong enough to overrule how rare it was to begin with — that's the entire arithmetic behind the 9%. Skipping the base rate isn't a small oversight. It's answering a different question than the one actually in front of you.

## The one question

So the next time a single case feels like proof, pause and ask: **what did I believe about this before I saw it?** That belief was already carrying most of the answer.

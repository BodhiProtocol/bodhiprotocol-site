A teacher asks a class to vote by raised hands. Normal voting works because each hand goes up once, gets counted once, and stays up long enough to be seen. Now imagine one student who keeps their hand shooting up and down, over and over, dozens of times in the ten seconds before the teacher finishes counting — not because they keep changing their mind, but because a vote count that has to watch a blur of motion instead of a steady hand is a vote count that can be pushed around by whoever generates the most motion, not whoever actually wants what they're voting for.

[Spoofing](/essays/what-spoofing-actually-looks-like) is a cleaner, more deliberate version of that trick — one fake order (or a handful), built to look like real demand, cancelled the instant it's done its job. This is the messier sibling: nobody needs any single order to be a lie. What does the damage is sheer volume and timing — a flood of order entries and cancellations concentrated in exactly the window where an exchange calculates an official reference price, dense enough that the calculation itself gets dragged around by traffic instead of genuine intent to trade.

## Why a reference price is even more exposed than an order book

Most of the trading day, price is just whatever the last trade happened to be — noisy, but hard to fake without actually buying or selling. A **closing price**, though, usually isn't "the last trade." Exchanges compute it deliberately, from an **auction**: orders accumulate for a few minutes, an **indicative price** updates continuously as those orders shift, and only at the very end does the exchange lock in a final print from whatever the book looks like at that moment.

That indicative price is the exposed part. It moves in response to *resting* orders, not just filled ones — which means someone who enters and modifies or cancels large orders fast enough, right inside that narrow window, can walk the indicative print around without a single one of those orders ever needing to trade. **Marking the close** is the name for deliberately doing this to benefit a position that settles against the closing price. **Quote stuffing** is the broader version — flooding order traffic at a rate designed to overwhelm a market's own systems or the traders watching it, whether or not the close specifically is the target.

## Example 1: International — the algorithm that made 70% of the volume disappear into cancels

On October 16, 2014, the SEC settled with Athena Capital Research LLC — the first market-manipulation case the agency had ever brought against a high-frequency trading firm. Between June and December 2009, Athena ran an algorithm internally code-named **Gravy**, timed to the final seconds of the Nasdaq trading day. Because Athena could predict, with better than 98% accuracy, that its own imbalance-on-close orders would get filled, it used that certainty to unleash a flood of buy or sell orders across thousands of stocks in the last moments before the close — traffic so dense it made up more than 70% of the total Nasdaq volume of the affected stocks in the seconds before the bell. The point was never any single order. It was generating enough one-sided traffic, in a window too narrow for anyone else to react to, that the official closing print moved to wherever Athena's position needed it.

## Example 2: India — a brand-new defense, tested on almost its first day

SEBI rebuilt exactly this exposure into a new mechanism starting August 3, 2026: the **Closing Auction Session (CAS)**. Continuous trading in covered stocks now halts at 3:15 pm; a reference price — the **Indicative Equilibrium Price (IEP)** — is calculated and updated between 3:15 and 3:20 pm from the auction order book; the auction runs from 3:20 pm with a randomized close between 3:28 and 3:30 pm, precisely so nobody can time a final push to a fixed second.

Ten days after the framework went live, on the Sensex weekly-expiry day of August 13, 2026, SEBI found three sharp spikes in the Sensex IEP during that same call window: the index jumping 362 points in two seconds at 15:20:41, another 133 points between 15:24:08 and 15:24:20, and a third jump of 405 points between 15:25:49 and 15:26:17 — all tied, SEBI's order says, to expiry-day Sensex options positions that would profit from exactly those moves. SEBI issued an ex-parte interim order against Copthall Mauritius Investment Limited and Mansi Share and Stock Broking Private Limited, estimating wrongful gains of roughly ₹2.96 crore and ₹71.65 lakh — and closed the investigation in six days, fast enough that it stands as SEBI's first enforcement action under a framework barely ten days old at the time of the trade.

The CAS was built specifically to make this harder than it had been under the old closing methodology. That it still got tested — and caught — within its first two weeks says less about the design failing and more about how immediately every new reference-price mechanism becomes the next target the moment it exists.

## Why it matters for a BA or QA

A surveillance rule that only inspects individual orders for suspicious size or price misses this pattern completely — Athena's orders and, per SEBI's order, the Sensex-session orders weren't necessarily large or badly priced one at a time. What has to be monitored is **traffic density inside the reference-price window itself**: the rate of order entry, modification, and cancellation per second, concentrated specifically inside a call auction or closing-cross period, correlated against a position that would profit from the print moving in that direction. That's a different query than "flag this order" — it's "flag this account's message rate over this ninety-second window," which most order-management systems don't compute by default and have to be built to.

It also means a reference-price calculation itself needs defenses, not just the surveillance layer watching it: minimum resting-time requirements before an order counts toward an indicative price, outlier dampening so one account's burst can't move the print past what the rest of the book supports, and — as CAS itself does — randomizing exactly when the window closes, so nobody can time a final push to a fixed second. SEBI closing this case in six days is the payoff of the same principle [the cancel/amend/delete essay](/essays/cancel-amend-delete-what-changes-the-moment-a-trade-executes) and [its companion on correction abuse](/essays/the-correction-that-isnt-cancel-amend-and-money-laundering) both rest on: every order, modification, and cancellation stays on a timestamped record nobody can edit away, which is what let SEBI reconstruct three two-second spikes down to the millisecond, nine years and one ocean away from an algorithm called Gravy doing the same thing in a plainer, slower market.

## Lighthouse insight

Go back to the classroom one more time. The teacher doesn't need to prove any single raised hand was fake to know something's wrong with a vote that flickers a hundred times in the last ten seconds — the flicker itself is the tell, independent of whether any individual hand meant it. A closing auction works exactly the same way: the defense was never going to be "catch the one bad order." It was always going to be watching the traffic itself, in the ten seconds that matter most, closely enough to notice when a steady hand turns into a blur.

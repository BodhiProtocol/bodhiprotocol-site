An airline is worried jet fuel will get more expensive.

A hedge fund wants to short a stock it thinks is overvalued.

A multinational needs to convert a quarter's revenue from dollars into euros.

Three different clients. Three different problems. The bank routes all three to a different desk, because no single trader can price fuel, equity borrow, and currency risk with the same instinct.

That is the entire reason trading desks exist: markets are too specialized for one generalist to run, so a bank organizes expertise by asset class and lets each desk become fluent in one corner of risk.

**A trading desk is not a bet. It is a specialized intermediary between a client's problem and the market's price.**

## Why desks exist

A trading desk sits between the client and the market.

The client wants to transact — buy, sell, hedge, finance, or exit a position. The market is a scatter of other participants, each with their own price and their own appetite. Someone has to stand in the middle, quote a firm price, take the position onto the bank's own book, and manage the risk until it can be laid off or run down.

That someone is the desk.

Desks earn for doing this in a few ways: the bid-ask spread, financing fees, structuring fees, commissions, prime brokerage fees. But every one of those revenue lines comes with inventory risk attached. A desk that quotes a price and gets hit has to hedge, or it is no longer a market maker — it is just a directional bet with extra steps.

**Trading desks are the market-making engine of an investment bank. Everything else is detail.**

## FX desk

The foreign exchange desk trades currency pairs: EUR/USD, USD/JPY, USD/INR.

Its clients are rarely making a bet on currencies for its own sake. A multinational converting dollar revenue into euros for a European subsidiary isn't speculating — it has a payroll to make. An Indian exporter selling in dollars wants next quarter's rupee proceeds to be predictable, not a lottery ticket.

The FX desk quotes, executes, and — because currencies are one of the deepest, most liquid markets on earth — does it at a spread thin enough that clients barely notice the machinery underneath.

## Equities desk

The equities desk trades stocks, ETFs, options, and large blocks of shares.

Its hardest problem isn't pricing. It's size. If a pension fund wants to buy a large stake in a company, doing it carelessly moves the price against the fund before the order is even filled. The equities desk's real product is discretion — algorithms and execution strategies that get a large order done without the market front-running it.

## Rates desk

The rates desk trades government bonds, interest-rate swaps, and rate futures.

Nothing on this desk moves without a central bank in the background. A company carrying floating-rate debt might hedge against a rate hike here. A bond fund might position ahead of an inflation print. Because interest rates set the price of almost everything else — mortgages, corporate loans, currency values — the rates desk is arguably the most systemically connected desk on the floor.

## Credit desk

The credit desk trades corporate bonds, credit default swaps, and distressed debt.

Its central risk is default: will the borrower pay this back? When the market starts doubting a company's ability to repay, its bond price falls and its credit spread widens — often before the equity market fully reacts. Credit traders live inside that gap between "the company hasn't defaulted yet" and "the market has already stopped believing it won't."

## Commodities desk

The commodities desk trades oil, gas, metals, and agricultural products.

Its clients look nothing like the clients on other desks. An airline hedges jet fuel. A mining company hedges the metal it digs up. A utility hedges electricity and gas costs months out. None of them care about commodities as an asset class — they care about not being blindsided by a price swing in the one input their business can't avoid.

## Prime brokerage

Prime brokerage isn't a desk in the trading sense. It's the infrastructure that makes the other desks usable by hedge funds.

Financing, securities lending, trade clearing, custody, margin reporting — if a hedge fund wants to short a stock, the shares it borrows to sell usually come through prime brokerage. Trading desks help clients transact. Prime brokerage helps clients operate at all.

## Sales vs. trading: the split inside every desk

Every one of these desks runs on a division most outsiders never notice: sales owns the client, trading owns the price.

A salesperson talks to the client, understands what they actually need, and carries the order to the desk. The trader doesn't manage the relationship — they decide what price the bank is willing to quote, and how the bank will hedge the position it just took on.

A pension fund calling in a large corporate bond order is a clean example. The salesperson translates the fund's need into an order. The credit trader prices it, executes it, and immediately starts managing the risk that trade just put on the bank's book.

Sales brings in the flow. Trading turns the flow into an executable, hedged price.

## Lighthouse insight

Every desk on this list is solving the same underlying problem with different collateral: a client has risk they don't want, and the bank is willing to hold that risk temporarily, for a price.

FX holds currency risk. Rates holds interest-rate risk. Credit holds default risk. Commodities holds input-price risk. Equities holds market risk. Prime brokerage holds none of the market risk — it holds the operational risk of letting someone else take positions at all.

That's the whole shape of a bank's markets business: client needs become market prices, market prices become hedged positions, and hedged positions become the liquidity everyone else in the market gets to trade against.

Trading desks are not where a bank gambles. They're where a bank gets paid to be the one person in the room who has to answer, right now, what something is worth.

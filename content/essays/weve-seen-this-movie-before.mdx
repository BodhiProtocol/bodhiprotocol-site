Imagine standing on a busy street in 1905.

A strange new machine rolls past.

It has an engine instead of a horse.

But everything else looks familiar.

The seats.

The wheels.

The shape.

Even the name.

People called it a *horseless carriage.*

The greatest invention of its generation wasn't imagined as something entirely new.

It was imagined as a better version of something old.

History has a habit of doing that.

Every revolutionary technology arrives wearing the clothes of the one before it.

When electricity replaced steam engines, many factories simply swapped the power source and kept the same layout. It took years before manufacturers realized electric motors no longer needed a single central engine. Factories could be redesigned from the ground up.

When movies first appeared, filmmakers didn't invent cinema.

They pointed a camera at a stage.

When the internet arrived, many companies built digital brochures instead of digital businesses.

Different centuries.

Different technologies.

The same instinct.

We borrow yesterday's mental model to understand tomorrow's invention.

Because imagining a better tool is easy.

Imagining a completely different world is hard.

## The Carriage Phase

This pattern appears so often that it deserves a name.

I call it **The Carriage Phase.**

It's the period when technology moves faster than imagination.

When we judge a new invention by how well it imitates the old world instead of asking what new world it makes possible.

The first automobile imitates a carriage.

The first movie imitates a theater.

The first website imitates a brochure.

The first smartphone imitates a computer.

The invention changes.

The mental model doesn't.

Not yet.

The slowest part of every technological revolution isn't engineering.

It's psychology.

History suggests we're standing in another carriage phase today.

Now think about AI.

Millions of people use it every day.

They write emails.

Generate code.

Summarize meetings.

Create presentations.

Answer questions.

Organizations proudly announce their AI initiatives.

And they should.

These are meaningful improvements.

But notice the question we're asking.

**"How can AI help us do what we already do?"**

It's the same question every generation asks at the beginning of a technological revolution.

History suggests the real breakthrough arrives when society asks a different question.

**"If this technology had always existed, would we design our world this way at all?"**

That question transformed factories after electricity.

It transformed cities after automobiles.

It transformed commerce after the internet.

None of those revolutions happened because the technology suddenly became more powerful.

They happened because people finally abandoned assumptions that no longer made sense.

AI may follow the same path.

Its biggest impact may not come from writing better emails.

Or generating better code.

Or answering more questions.

It may come from making entire ways of working feel as outdated as a horse carriage on a modern highway.

Picture a company that doesn't use AI to answer support tickets faster, but stops having a support queue at all — because the product now explains itself before anyone has to ask.

One day, people won't ask,

**"How can AI help us work?"**

They'll ask,

**"Why did work ever look like this?"**

That won't be the moment AI changes.

It will be the moment **our assumptions change.**

History suggests that's when every real revolution truly begins.

## 💡 Insight

Every technological revolution happens twice.

The first revolution invents a new machine.

The second revolution invents a new mindset.

Most people remember the invention.

History remembers the reinvention.
